# Part 4 — The Merchants and the Lawgiver
## SEVENTH DRAFT — v0.7
**Date:** November 16, 2026
**Status:** Rewritten to the draft-07 brief. Two stories in order: a merchant family (c. 1900 BCE), then Mari and Hammurabi (1790s–1750s), then the chariot and the raid on Babylon (1595), ending on the Aegean. Title changed from "The Middle Bronze Age World".
**Driving questions:** How do you mail a letter before paper? What is a loan before banks, and what happens when you can't pay? Where does law come from? Why did the wheel take so long?

---

## The Draft

---

[VISUAL: Part card — WHEN: 1900 BCE, a century after Ur fell. WHERE: Assur, on the Tigris in northern Iraq, and Kanesh, in central Turkey. THE FAMILY: this is the Carrier's hour.]

A woman is writing to her husband, and she's annoyed.

He's seven hundred miles away. He's been away for years. And in his last letter he complained about the cloth she's been sending him to sell. It's too small, he said. It isn't good enough.

Her answer survives. I'll give you the gist, because I don't trust myself with the exact words. She tells him she made the pieces smaller because he told her to. Now he writes and says to put more wool in each one. Fine. She's done it. And she'd like to know who is in his house criticizing her cloth.

Her name is Lamassi. His is Pushu-ken. They lived about three thousand nine hundred years ago, and we have dozens of their letters, and those are the first private voices in this story. Up to now, everything I've read you was written by a temple or a king. This is a married couple arguing about work.

[VISUAL: Object — an Old Assyrian letter beside its broken clay envelope (e.g., Metropolitan Museum).]

So, first question. How do you mail a letter when there's no paper?

You take a lump of clean clay about the size of your palm and you write on it with the cut end of a reed while it's damp. You let it dry. Then you wrap it in a thin second skin of clay, like a pie crust, and on the outside you write who it's for and roll your personal seal across it. That's the envelope. To read the letter you have to break it, so if it arrives cracked, somebody's been in your mail. Archaeologists have found some that were never opened. They can X-ray them now and read the letter inside.

Then you hand it to a man with a donkey.

Lamassi lives in a city called Assur, on the Tigris River in northern Iraq. Pushu-ken lives in a town called Kanesh, on the high plateau in the middle of Turkey. Between them are six weeks of walking and a range of mountains. The letter goes with the next caravan, and the answer comes back with the one after that. Ask a question in spring and you'll have your reply by autumn.

[VISUAL: Map — the route from Assur north-west over the Taurus Mountains to Kanesh; "700 miles, six weeks."]

What are these people doing so far apart?

Remember the problem. Bronze needs tin, and tin comes from far away. Around 1900 BCE the tin is coming from somewhere deep in Asia, by way of Iran, and it arrives in Assur. Meanwhile, the kingdoms of central Turkey have copper, and silver, and gold, and no tin at all.

Assur is sitting between a place with tin and a place that will pay nearly anything for it.

So the merchants of Assur buy tin, and they buy fine wool cloth, some of it woven by their own wives and daughters. They pack it on donkeys. A donkey carries about a hundred and fifty pounds: tin in sealed bags on each side, cloth on top. They walk it seven hundred miles to Kanesh. And there the tin sells for about twice what they paid for it, and the cloth for three times.

That's the whole business. Double your money on tin, triple it on cloth, carry the silver home, and do it again. A man might do it for thirty years and never go home at all, which is why his wife is writing to him.

It was big. Going by the surviving records, the caravans moved something like a hundred tons of tin and a hundred thousand bolts of cloth in a single generation. Every pound of it on the back of a donkey.

This is the Carrier's line, and look how far it's come. In the first house he was the brother who walked to get volcanic glass. In Uruk he pulled a barge for the temple. Now he's in business for himself.

[VISUAL: Illustration — the merchant quarter at Kanesh below the citadel mound; houses with archive rooms.]

And the setup at the far end is the cleverest part.

The traders from Assur didn't conquer Kanesh. They couldn't have. They rented. They had a neighborhood at the foot of the hill, below the local king's palace, and inside that neighborhood they ran their own affairs. They had their own courts, their own rules, their own assembly. They paid the local king his taxes and gave him first pick of the goods, and in return he promised to protect the caravans and to pay compensation if one was robbed on his roads. It was written into treaties.

I think of it as an embassy with a homeowners' association.

Now here's what makes these people worth an hour. Assur, the home city, wasn't run by a king in the usual sense. It had a ruler, but his title was "steward of the god," and the real decisions were made by an assembly of the heads of the big merchant families. Each year was named after an official who was picked by drawing lots. It was a small trading republic, four thousand years ago, and almost nobody has heard of it, because it never built a pyramid. It left paperwork.

Twenty-three thousand clay tablets have come out of the houses at Kanesh, and nearly all of them are from two generations of these families. The quarter burned around 1830 BCE, the fire baked the tablets hard, and there they sat.

Second question. What's a loan, before there are banks?

It's a loan. The contracts name the amount, the due date and the interest. Among these merchants the standard rate was thirty percent a year. That's credit-card money, and they paid it gladly, because the trade earned more.

They had something more sophisticated too. A dozen investors would each put gold into what they called a sack. One trader ran the sack for ten or twelve years. At the end he kept a third of the profit and the investors split the rest. If an investor wanted out early, he got his money back and nothing more. That is a venture fund, with a lockup period, in 1900 BCE.

They had debt notes you could hand on to someone else to collect. They had partners, and agents, and lawsuits when partners cheated. A Dutch scholar who spent his life on these tablets wrote an article about them with the phrase "modern features" in the title, and I can't improve on that.

[VISUAL: Map detail — the main road with a toll station; a faint side path through the hills marked "the narrow track."]

And they cheated on their taxes.

The local kings charged a toll on everything that came through. So there was a back road through the hills that bypassed the toll stations. The letters call it the narrow track, and they discuss it the way you'd discuss a shipping option. Is it worth it this season? Are the guards watching it?

Sometimes it went wrong. There's a letter in the archive from a merchant to his partner, warning him off. It says, in effect: so-and-so's son sent contraband to Pushu-ken, and it was caught, and the palace has seized Pushu-ken and put him in jail. The guards are strict. Please don't try to smuggle anything.

So Lamassi's husband, the one who complained about her cloth, did time.

I love that. And I think it tells you something serious. You only cheat a system that's worth being in. Smuggling means the system is working. Remember, too, that the whole thing ran on other kings' land, by treaty. It was a permission, and permissions can be taken back. We'll see what happens to a world built on those.

[VISUAL: Illustration — a courtyard house in Assur: looms, children, a woman holding a tablet.]

Now look at the other end, because the men in Kanesh are only half the firm.

Lamassi is in Assur running everything else. She buys the wool. She manages the women who weave it, and she weaves herself. A good piece takes months. She arranges the donkeys, pays the export tax, deals with her husband's creditors, and raises the children, and she does it for years at a stretch with a husband who is a six-week walk away.

The wives' letters are the best things in the archive. They're sharp. One woman tells her husband that he left her in an empty house without so much as a quart of barley while he builds himself a fine new one out there. Another writes something close to this: you love money and you hate your own life. Come home.

They had reasons to worry. The law of these merchants allowed a man one wife in Assur and a second in Turkey, provided it was only one in each place. The contracts spell it out.

And did the wives write these letters themselves? The script the merchants used was a stripped-down version, a couple of hundred signs, enough for letters and accounts. A scholar named Cécile Michel has studied the women's letters more closely than anyone, and she thinks a good number of them were written by the women's own hands. That would make this the first place on Earth where reading and writing got loose from the temple and the palace. The Counter's skill, invented to track a god's barley, is being used by a wife to tell her husband he's an idiot.

One thing I said I wouldn't let slide. In the same archive rooms, in the same handwriting, there are bills of sale for people. A girl, bought for so much silver, with witnesses. Families in debt handing over a child. Lamassi's weaving women may have been hired, or may have been owned. The letters don't say which, because to the people writing them it wasn't an interesting difference.

And there's one small thing in these letters to tuck away for later. Now and then the merchants mention a metal so rare that the city of Assur restricted who could trade it. It was worth forty times its weight in silver. Most scholars think it was iron, probably the kind that falls from the sky in meteorites. In 1900 BCE iron is jewelry.

[VISUAL: Date card — 1750 BCE. Map: Ur, on the Gulf. Object: the complaint tablet to Ea-nasir (British Museum).]

Go forward a hundred and fifty years, and south to the old city of Ur, because I can't do a part about merchants without the worst one.

His name was Ea-nasir, and he dealt in copper. He bought it at an island in the Persian Gulf and sold it in Ur. And in the ruins of what was probably his house, archaeologists found a tablet from a customer named Nanni.

Nanni had paid in advance for good copper. What he was offered, he says, was junk. He sent a servant to sort it out and Ea-nasir was rude to the man and sent him away, twice, through dangerous country. Nanni writes: "What do you take me for, that you treat somebody like me with such contempt?" And he says that from now on he'll pick out the copper himself, in his own yard, and he'll exercise his right to reject it.

That tablet is in the British Museum, and it's recognized as the oldest customer complaint in the world. A few years ago the internet found it, and Ea-nasir is now more famous than nearly every king in this series.

Here's why I think it deserves that. It wasn't the only one. Other letters from other unhappy customers were found in the same house. He kept them. And for that complaint to exist at all, a great deal has to be in place. Payment in advance. An agreed standard of quality. A messenger service. A right to refuse delivery. Nanni is furious because he believes the system owes him something. In four thousand years of records, that's about the most modern feeling I can find.

So that's what a loan and a deal look like. Now the darker half of the question. What happens when you can't pay?

[VISUAL: Diagram — a farmer's year: borrow grain in spring (price high), repay at harvest (price low), "plus one third."]

Come back to the Farmer, because he's a borrower too, and he isn't borrowing to triple his money.

He borrows grain in the spring, when last year's has run out and this year's isn't ripe. He pays it back at harvest. In the south, around Babylon, the going rate for a silver loan was twenty percent. For a grain loan it was a third. The poor man's loan cost more than the merchant's, and he was paying it back at the one time of year when grain was cheapest. That's a payday loan, and it's almost four thousand years old.

If the harvest is poor, he can't pay. Then the lender takes what the loan was secured on. First the ox. Then the field. Then a child, sent to work in the lender's house until the debt is cleared, which with interest it never is. Then the wife. Then himself.

Run that for a generation and you get a country where a few rich households own most of the land and most of the people. And that's dangerous for a king, because farmers who have lost their land don't pay taxes and don't turn up for the army.

So the kings did something I find astonishing. Every so often, usually when a new king took the throne, he announced that the debts were cancelled. All the small personal ones, the grain loans and the silver loans to farmers. Anyone who'd been handed over for debt went home. The fields went back. The clay tablets the debts were written on were broken. Business loans between merchants weren't touched, because those were investments.

We have the text of some of these decrees. They happened again and again, for centuries. The kings called it "establishing justice."

It was a reset button. A thousand years later you find the same idea in the Bible, as the Jubilee. I think it's one of the most interesting inventions of the whole Bronze Age, and hardly anyone has heard of it, because the king we remember from this period is remembered for something else.

[VISUAL: Map — Mesopotamia c. 1790 BCE as a patchwork of rival kingdoms: Babylon small in the center, Mari on the Euphrates, Aleppo, Larsa.]

When he took the throne in 1792 BCE, Babylon was a small kingdom in the middle of the plain with bigger neighbors on every side.

We know what the map looked like because of a letter. It was written by an official to his king, and it says: there is no king who is strong on his own. Ten or fifteen kings follow the king of Babylon. As many follow the king of Larsa. As many follow two others. And twenty follow the king of Aleppo.

That's the world of 1770 BCE in one paragraph. Nobody is on top. Everyone needs allies.

That letter was found in a city called Mari, on the Euphrates in eastern Syria, and Mari is where I want to take you, because its archive is as good as the merchants', except that it's the kings' mail.

Some of it is very human. A few years earlier Mari had been conquered by a tough old warlord from the north, who put his younger son on its throne. The son turned out to like wine and women more than work. And we have the father's letters. In one he writes: are you a child? Is there no beard on your chin? He points out that while the boy lies around among the women, his older brother is out winning battles.

That's a king, in 1780 BCE, writing the letter every disappointed father has wanted to write.

When the old man died, Mari was taken back by the family he'd thrown out. The new king was named Zimri-Lim, and he's one of the most likable rulers in the ancient world. His palace had more than two hundred and sixty rooms and painted walls, and visitors came from other kingdoms just to see it. His letters are full of his daily business. A lion was caught on a rooftop in one of his towns, and the governor wrote to ask what to do with it. He built an ice house, to have cold drinks in summer. His wife ran the palace when he travelled and wrote to him about everything.

And his great ally was the king of Babylon. For years they exchanged troops, and intelligence, and gifts. Zimri-Lim's soldiers fought in the king of Babylon's wars. His ambassadors at the Babylonian court sent home reports of what the king had said at dinner.

[VISUAL: Photo — the excavated palace of Mari from the air. Date card: 1761 BCE.]

Then, late in his reign, the king of Babylon made his move. In about five years he knocked out his rivals one by one. Larsa in the south. The kingdom to the east. And in 1761 he turned on Mari.

We don't know what happened to Zimri-Lim. He just stops. What we know is that the Babylonian army occupied the palace, and that Babylonian clerks then spent months going through the archive. They sorted the tablets into baskets and labelled them. We have the labels. They took away the letters they wanted, which seem to have been the ones from other great kings. Then, two years later, the soldiers came back, stripped the palace, and burned it.

The fire baked what was left. In 1933 some men digging a grave on a hill by the Euphrates hit a stone statue, and the French archaeologists who followed found twenty-five thousand tablets in the ruins. Everything I've just told you about the lion and the ice house and the lazy son comes out of that fire.

The king who burned his friend's palace was Hammurabi.

[VISUAL: Object — the stele of Hammurabi (Louvre); zoom to the king before the seated sun god; then the columns of text.]

Third question. Where does law come from?

You've probably heard that it comes from him. Near the end of his life he had a pillar of black stone set up, seven feet tall. At the top it shows him standing before the god of the sun, who is the god of justice. Below are two hundred and eighty-two rulings, carved in fine script. It's in the Louvre in Paris.

"An eye for an eye" is in there. But read it closely. If a gentleman puts out the eye of another gentleman, he loses his own eye. If he puts out the eye of a commoner, he pays a fine. If it's a slave's eye, he pays the owner half the slave's price. Equal justice between equals, and a price list for everyone else.

A lot of it is about ordinary trouble. A builder puts up a house, it falls down and kills the owner, and the builder is put to death. A surgeon whose patient dies loses his hand. A man who can't pay his debts may hand over his wife or his child to work it off, and here's a line I want you to hear: they serve three years, and in the fourth they go free. That's the same sickness the debt cancellations were treating.

So was this the law? I don't think so, not the way we mean it. We have thousands of court records from his reign and the years after, and not one judge ever cites that stone. It wasn't the first collection of its kind either. We met one three hundred years older. What the pillar really is, I think, is an advertisement. It's a king at the end of his life saying: look at the kind of order I kept.

Hammurabi is famous for the wrong thing. The stone made him the great lawgiver. The cancelled debts did more for more people, and so did the dull habit of writing down what was decided. And the man himself spent his best years as a schemer who waited thirty years and then ate his neighbors.

One more thing about that pillar. It didn't stay in Babylon. Six hundred years later a king from Iran carried it off as a trophy, and that's where it was dug up. Keep that in mind. We'll meet the thief.

[VISUAL: Map — Sinai Peninsula, the turquoise mines marked. Object: a Proto-Sinaitic inscription; highlight the ox-head sign; morph it by stages into the letter A.]

Before we leave this century I need to show you something small that happened off to the side, in the desert, because it turns out to be the biggest thing in this whole part.

Around 1850 BCE, while Lamassi's grandchildren were still running donkeys, the Egyptians were mining turquoise in the Sinai. They brought in foreign workers to do it, people from Canaan who spoke a language related to Hebrew. Those workers saw Egyptian picture-writing on every monument around them. They couldn't read it. Egyptian writing took years of school, like the wedge script in Iraq.

But somebody in that camp had an idea. Take a picture and use it for the first sound of the word, in your own language. Their word for ox was something like "alef." So a drawing of an ox's head means the sound at the start of alef. Their word for house was "bet." A little floor plan of a house means B.

They scratched short messages on the rocks around the mine that way, with two dozen signs. They're still there.

Turn a capital A upside down. You'll see the horns.

That's all I'll say for now. The people who did that were miners, and by the standards of every scribe in Egypt they couldn't write. Hold onto the ox.

[VISUAL: Diagram — a solid plank wheel and a spoked wheel side by side; then a map of the steppe east of the Ural Mountains. Date card: 2000 BCE.]

Fourth question, and it sets up the rest of the series. Why did the wheel take so long?

It seems like the most obvious invention there is, and people had been watching logs roll for a few hundred thousand years. But the wheel was never the hard part. The axle was. A wheel has to turn freely on a fixed rod without wobbling and without sticking. That takes a round hole and a round peg that fit each other closely, in hard wood, and to cut those you need metal tools. You also need something worth carting, an animal trained to pull, and ground flat enough to roll on. All of that came together around 3500 BCE, and the first wheels were solid discs made of planks, on heavy carts pulled by oxen at walking pace.

For a wheel to be fast it has to be light, and that means spokes: a rim, a hub, and thin rods between. That's fine woodworking, and it appears about 2000 BCE. It doesn't appear in any of the cities. It shows up on the grasslands east of the Ural Mountains, among herders who had no writing and no kings. They buried their dead with two-wheeled carts, and the wheels left their prints in the soil of the graves, spokes and all. Those same people had been breeding a new, stronger kind of horse.

A light cart, two spoked wheels, two fast horses. Put a driver in it and an archer beside him. That's a chariot, and nothing on foot can catch it or get away from it.

Over the next three centuries it spread south into every kingdom we've met. And think about what it takes to keep one. A horse trainer. A stable. A wheelwright, a joiner, a harness maker. An archer who's practiced since he was a boy. A chariot is like a fighter jet. A farmer can't own one and a village can't either. Only a palace can.

So the Maker gets a new trade: he builds chariots. And the Keeper gets a weapon that only he can afford. From here on, war belongs to kings, and every king needs a rich palace to pay for his chariots. Keep that in mind for when the palaces start to fail.

[VISUAL: Map — arrow from central Turkey down the Euphrates to Babylon and back. Date card: 1595 BCE.]

The chariot announced itself in 1595 BCE.

Up in central Turkey, in the same country where the merchants of Assur once had their quarter, a new kingdom had formed. We call its people the Hittites. In 1595 their king led his army out of the highlands, down the length of the Euphrates, about eight hundred miles, and sacked Babylon. Hammurabi's city, five generations after Hammurabi. Then he turned round and went home with the loot, and his brother-in-law murdered him.

He didn't try to hold Babylon. He couldn't have. But everyone took note. An army could now arrive from a place that wasn't on your map.

Babylon was picked up by a mountain people called the Kassites, who ruled it for the next four hundred years and became more Babylonian than the Babylonians. Egypt went through the same lesson: chariot-driving foreigners from Canaan took over the Nile Delta for a century, the Egyptians threw them out around 1550, and they kept the chariots. The Egypt that comes out of that war is the one with the famous names, and it has an army and wants to use it.

[VISUAL: Map — the Aegean: Crete, the island of Thera, mainland Greece. Photo: a wall painting from Akrotiri. Date card: around 1600 BCE.]

And there's one more part of the map I've been neglecting, and it's the part you probably know best.

On the island of Crete, by this time, there's a civilization with palaces. The biggest is at a place called Knossos. It had hundreds of rooms on several floors around a central court, with storerooms and workshops and stairways. It was an administrative center, like the temple at Uruk. They kept accounts on clay in a script nobody has ever deciphered. They painted their walls with dolphins and with young men and women vaulting over the backs of bulls. Later Greeks would remember Crete as the home of a king with a maze under his house and a monster in it that was half bull.

Seventy miles north of Crete was an island with a town on it, and around 1600 BCE the island blew up. It was one of the largest volcanic eruptions of the last ten thousand years. The middle of the island collapsed into the sea. The town was buried under ash, the way Pompeii would be seventeen centuries later.

Archaeologists have been digging that town since the 1960s. The houses stand three stories high. There are wall paintings as fresh as the day they were covered: ships, swallows, boys boxing, a fisherman holding up his catch. There are indoor toilets connected to drains under the street.

There are no bodies, and no jewelry either. The earthquakes came first, and the people understood, and they packed up what they could carry and left before the end. Where they went, we don't know.

Crete lived on for a few generations, weaker. And on the mainland of Greece a harder people were watching. At a hilltop called Mycenae they were burying their chiefs in deep shafts with gold masks over their faces and bronze swords at their sides, and around their necks, beads of amber that had come all the way from the Baltic Sea. Before long they'd cross to Crete and move into the palace at Knossos, and they'd take the Cretans' script and bend it to write their own language.

Their own language was Greek. These are the people who, in a much later poem, sail to Troy.

[VISUAL: Part card update — THE FAMILY, 1500 BCE: the Carrier, a merchant with partners and lawsuits; the Counter, anyone who can pay for lessons; the Keeper, a lawgiver with chariots; the Maker, building them; the Farmer, in debt.]

So where's the family, around 1500 BCE?

The Carrier is a businessman. He has investors, contracts, a tax strategy, and a wife who runs the home office.

The Counter isn't only a temple clerk now. He might be a merchant, or a merchant's wife, or a miner scratching an ox's head on a rock.

The Keeper has learned to cancel debts, to carve his justice in stone, and to burn his friend's house down. He has chariots.

The Maker builds them.

And the Farmer is in debt. Sometimes a king wipes it out. Then it builds up again.

They live in different countries now and they'll never meet. And notice that none of them can do without the others. The Keeper needs the Maker's chariots. The Maker needs the Carrier's tin. The Carrier needs the Counter's contracts and the Keeper's treaties. All of them eat the Farmer's barley.

In about a hundred and fifty years they'll have built the most connected world there has ever been. I want to show it to you from the deck of a ship.
