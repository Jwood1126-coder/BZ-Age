[VISUAL: Part card — WHEN: around 1300 BCE. WHERE: off the south coast of Turkey. THE FAMILY: all five, for once, in the same place.]

Off the southern coast of Turkey, around the year 1300 BCE, a ship is going down.

She isn't big. About fifty feet, the length of a city bus and a bit. She's built of cedar from the mountains of Lebanon, and she's riding very low, because she's carrying ten tons of copper.

It's in three hundred and fifty-four slabs, more than fifty pounds each, cast flat with a handle at every corner so two men can carry one between them. They're stacked in overlapping rows like roof tiles. Under them is a ton of tin. Around them are a hundred and fifty big clay jars, most of them full of a sticky tree resin used for incense. There are logs of black wood from Africa. There are elephant tusks and hippopotamus teeth. There are a hundred and seventy-five round cakes of raw glass, most of them deep blue. There are ostrich eggs, and beads of amber from the Baltic Sea. There's a gold scarab with the name of the queen of Egypt on it.

Picture the practical problem of working around that cargo. Each slab is roughly the weight of a heavy suitcase. Jars have to be secured against movement; loads need to be placed so the vessel can sail safely. This is a reconstruction of the work, but the weight is real. Those beautiful objects in museum cases were once freight that somebody had to lift.

And somewhere near the bow there's a small bronze statue of a goddess with gold over her face and hands. She's there to get the ship home.

We don't know exactly how the ship was lost. Her cargo came to rest down a steep underwater slope, where it remained for more than three thousand years. The wreck gives us objects and their positions; it cannot replay the last minutes of the voyage.

[VISUAL: Photo — the Uluburun copper ingots on the seabed. Then: the reconstructed hull section, Bodrum Museum.]

In the summer of 1982 a young Turkish sponge diver named Mehmet Çakır came up and told his captain he'd seen metal biscuits with ears on the bottom. The captain knew what that meant. Archaeologists spent eleven summers and more than twenty thousand dives bringing her up.

I'm starting here because that ship is the best single picture of this age. Go through the hold. The copper is from Cyprus. The glass is Egyptian. The black wood is from south of Egypt. The amber came from the far side of Europe. The jars are from the coast of Lebanon and Israel. Nothing on her was made anywhere near where she sank, and no one country on Earth could have filled her.

You've seen this ship. She docks at Rotterdam now, and she's a quarter of a mile long.

The wreck brings our five kinds of work together. The Carrier moves the cargo. The Maker needs its metal. A folding wooden notebook with an ivory hinge gives us the Counter's tool: its leaves were made to hold wax, ready for a pointed stylus. Figs, olives, and pomegranates bring us back to the Farmer. And eleven tons of bronze in the making would interest the Keeper of a royal storehouse. Many researchers think this was a shipment from one king to another.

People who might never meet depended on one another to put this cargo at sea. Now their work lies together on the seabed.

[VISUAL: Map — eastern Mediterranean with the counterclockwise sailing circuit: Egypt, the Levant coast, Cyprus, southern Turkey, the Aegean, Crete, North Africa, back to Egypt.]

First question. How do you sail something like that with no compass and no engine?

You stay in sight of land whenever you can. You steer by mountains in the day and by stars at night. You sail in summer, when the weather is steady, and you stay in port in winter. And you go one way round. The winds and currents at this end of the Mediterranean favor a circuit: up the coast from Egypt past Lebanon, west along Turkey, across to Greece and Crete, down to Africa, and back along the shore to the Nile. This ship was on the Turkey leg, heading west, when she was lost.

We even have a fair idea who was on her, from what they left. There were several sets of the little stone weights a merchant uses with a balance, and they're on the standard used in Syria and Canaan. So, three or four merchants from that coast. There were two swords and some personal gear of the kind used in Greece. So, perhaps two Greeks, and since they carried swords and not weights, they may have been escorts sent to see the cargo home.

And my favorite passenger: a mouse. The excavators found the jawbone of a house mouse in the sediment, and a specialist compared it with mouse populations around the Mediterranean. It matched the mice of the Syrian coast. So that's probably where the ship last took on cargo, and we know it because of a stowaway.

Now the tin. Researchers have compared the metal in these ingots with ores from different regions, using chemical composition and isotopes. A study published in 2022 proposed sources in Central Asia and the Taurus Mountains of Turkey; other researchers have challenged those assignments. The broad point is safer than a map with a precise route drawn on it. This was an international trade in material whose origins are difficult to reconstruct, even with the cargo in our hands.

We don't have the shipment's full paperwork. Its merchants may have bought through intermediaries rather than dealing with the miners themselves. The ingots preserve the metal, not every conversation that moved it toward the coast.

To understand who that cargo was for, I have to take you back about fifty years, and then we'll go forward in order.

[VISUAL: Map — the "table": Egypt (gold), Hatti in central Turkey, Mitanni in north Syria (horses), Babylon (blue stone), Assyria (small, in grey), Cyprus (copper), Mycenaean Greece (oil), Ugarit (a port). Date card: 1350 BCE.]

In 1350 BCE the world from Greece to Iran is run by a handful of great kings, and they know each other. Picture them round a table. I'll give each one a handle, and I'll use the same handle every time.

Egypt is the gold country. It has mines in the desert to the south that produce more gold than everywhere else combined, and it spends that gold on friends.

Across from Egypt, in central Turkey, is Hatti, the kingdom of the Hittites, the ones who raided Babylon. They called their own country the land of a thousand gods, because every time they conquered a people they adopted that people's gods too. They're Egypt's great rival.

Between them, in northern Syria, is Mitanni, the horse kingdom. Its kings trained the best chariot horses in the world, and here's a strange thing about them. A horse-training manual written by one of their experts counts the laps of the track in words that are close cousins of Sanskrit. A treaty of theirs is sworn by gods who also appear in the oldest hymns of India. Somehow a ruling family with roots far to the east ended up running a kingdom in Syria.

In the old country there's Babylon, under its mountain kings, selling horses and blue stone and living on its reputation as the oldest school in the world.

In northern Iraq is Assyria, a kingdom with its old center at Assur, the city of the donkey merchants. During these generations its rulers are emerging from Mitanni's dominance and asserting themselves among the great powers. Remember the distinction: Assur is the city; Assyria is the kingdom whose power is growing around it.

Out in the water is Alashiya, the copper island. That's Cyprus.

At the far western end are the Greeks of Mycenae, who export perfumed oil and are just starting to be taken seriously.

And where all the routes cross is a port on the Syrian coast called Ugarit. It isn't a great power. It's where the great powers' goods change hands. We'll spend an evening there.

[VISUAL: Object — an Amarna letter (British Museum or Berlin). Date card: found 1887.]

We know how these kings dealt with each other because of something that happened in 1887. A village woman in Egypt was digging in an old ruin for crumbled mud brick, which makes good fertilizer, and she turned up a pile of clay tablets. She sold them for next to nothing. They turned out to be the foreign mail of the king of Egypt: about three hundred and fifty letters to and from the other rulers of the world.

Second question. How do kings with no common language talk to each other?

Much of the correspondence was written in Babylonian, a form of Akkadian spoken in Mesopotamia. For courts such as Egypt's and the Hittites', it served as an international language learned by scribes. Think of aviation English: the shared language makes the exchange possible even when the people on either side speak different languages at home.

And they had rules. The great kings call each other "brother." It doesn't mean they like each other. It means "I recognize you as my equal." Small kings write to the great ones as "my lord, my sun," and say they throw themselves at his feet seven times and seven times more. You always know from the first line who outranks whom.

So what do the masters of the world write to each other about? Mostly three things.

The first is gold. Everyone wants Egypt's, and the phrase they all use is that in Egypt gold is as plentiful as dust. The king of the horse kingdom writes that the king of Egypt promised him statues of solid gold, and what arrived was wood with gold leaf over it. And the gold is like dust in your country, he says. So why did that cause you such pain?

The second is marriage, and here's the third question. How do you marry someone you've never seen, who lives three months away and doesn't speak your language?

There was a whole procedure. Envoys went to look the girl over. There was a ceremony where oil was poured on her head. There was a list of her dowry down to the last comb. Then she travelled, with an escort of hundreds, and she never went home again.

But it only went one way. Everyone sent daughters to Egypt. Egypt never sent one back. When the king of Babylon asked for an Egyptian princess, the answer came: "From time immemorial, no daughter of the king of Egypt is given to anyone." And the king of Babylon's reply is one of my favorite lines from the ancient world. Then send me any beautiful woman, he says, as if she were your daughter. Who's going to say she isn't a princess?

The same king complains that his sister was sent to Egypt years ago and his envoys can't get anyone to confirm she's still alive.

The third thing is complaints. You sent no gift. You sent a smaller gift than you sent my father. Your envoy was rude to my envoy. You kept my man waiting.

One of those complaints I can't leave out. The king of Babylon had been ill, and the king of Egypt hadn't sent to ask how he was. He was offended, and he says so. Then, he writes, his own envoy explained to him that Egypt is a very long way off, too far for the news to have got there and a get-well message to have come back. And the king says he asked the Egyptian envoy whether that was true, and it was, and so he isn't angry any more.

Think about that. One of the most powerful men alive had to ask a messenger how big the world was.

And there's a letter in that archive from the rising Assyrian kingdom in northern Iraq. Its king writes to Egypt for the first time, on his own account, to introduce himself and send a chariot and two white horses. The king of Babylon found out and was livid. Those people are my subjects, he wrote to Egypt. If you love me, send them home with nothing.

Egypt didn't. A new chair had been pulled up to the table.

[VISUAL: Object — a colossal statue of Akhenaten (Cairo) with the long face. Map: Amarna, halfway down the Nile. Date card: 1353 BCE.]

Now, the reason those letters survived is that they were filed in a city that was built in a hurry and abandoned in a hurry, and the man who built it is one of the strangest rulers in history.

He came to the throne of Egypt in 1353 BCE under the name Amenhotep, which means "the god Amun is content." Amun was the great god of Egypt. His temple was the richest institution in the country. It owned land and ships and tens of thousands of workers.

Within five years the new king had turned on him. He announced that there was one god worth worshipping, the disc of the sun, which he called the Aten. He changed his own name to Akhenaten, "useful to the Aten." He closed the old temples and sent men with chisels up and down Egypt to cut Amun's name out of the walls. Later they started cutting out the word "gods" where it appeared in the plural.

And he left the capital. He picked a stretch of empty desert by the Nile where nothing had ever stood, and he had a city built there for thirty thousand people in about four years. They did it by cutting the stone into small blocks that one man could carry on his shoulder.

People have wanted to make him a hero for a long time: the first man to believe in one god, a thousand years ahead of his age. Sigmund Freud wrote a book suggesting that Moses was one of his followers. More recently historians have gone the other way and called it a power grab, a king breaking the priests who were rich enough to rival him.

I don't fully buy either one. I think he believed it. The hymn to the sun that was written at his court reads like the work of someone who meant it. I also think it's no accident that his one god spoke only through him, and that the temples he closed were the only institution in Egypt that could tell a king no. Both can be true of the same man.

Now look away from the royal portraits and out to the city's cemeteries. Excavators have recovered hundreds of ordinary people. The burial grounds do not all tell the same story. At the North Tombs Cemetery, the sample is strikingly young: almost everyone whose age could be closely estimated died between five and twenty-five. Skeletal studies also reveal physical strain and poor health. They cannot tell us the exact load each person carried, but the contrast with the royal image of abundance is hard to miss.

That's another view of the city of the sun, from the people who had to live and work beneath it.

Meanwhile his empire in Syria was coming loose, and we can listen to it happen. His subject king at the port of Byblos, on the coast of Lebanon, wrote to him about sixty times. It's always the same letter. My neighbors are taking my villages. My farmers have run away. Send archers. Why don't you answer? I am like a bird in a trap, he says, here in Byblos.

At one point Egypt wrote back, and the reply amounts to: why do you write to me more than all my other governors put together?

No archers came. In the end his own brother locked him out of the city, and the letters stop.

Akhenaten died after seventeen years. Within a generation his city was empty, his temples had been pulled down, and his name had been cut out of the records so thoroughly that for three thousand years nobody knew he'd existed. The filing cabinet with the foreign letters was left behind in the empty office, and that's the only reason we have them.

[VISUAL: Object — the gold mask of Tutankhamun. Date card: 1323 BCE.]

He left a son, about nine years old. The boy had been named Tutankhaten, "living image of the Aten." His advisers changed it. They put the old god back, in the name and in the temples. He became Tutankhamun.

That's who he is. The gold mask everybody has seen belongs to the child of the heretic, whose whole short reign was spent undoing his father's.

He died at about nineteen, with no surviving children. And then the palace did something no one would have believed.

Up in Turkey, the king of the Hittites was on campaign in Syria when a messenger arrived from Egypt with a letter from a queen whose husband had just died. The Hittite records don't give her name. Most historians think she was Tutankhamun's widow, and I'll go with that. Her letter is quoted in those records. "My husband is dead. I have no son. They say you have many sons. Give me one of yours, and he will be my husband, and he will be king of Egypt."

The Hittite king didn't believe it. The records say he told his council that nothing like this had ever happened to him in his life. Egypt didn't even send out its daughters. Now it was offering him the throne. He sent a man to Egypt to find out whether it was a trick.

Her reply came back in the spring, and you can still hear how insulted she was. "Why do you say, they are deceiving me? If I had a son, would I have written to a foreign country in a way that humiliates me and my country?"

So he sent a son. And the prince died on the way. The Hittites said he was murdered, and they were probably right. There were men in Egypt who had no intention of taking orders from a foreigner, and one of them was on the throne within weeks.

Let me stop for a what-if, because it's one of the great ones. Suppose the prince had arrived. A Hittite on the throne of Egypt, with his father ruling Turkey and Syria. The two strongest states in the world would have been joined in one family. Would it have held? I doubt it. Egypt's priests and generals would have had a foreign boy in their hands, a long way from home. But it's the nearest those two powers ever came to becoming one, and it turned on whether a young man survived a journey. Nobody can know how it would have gone. What we know is that he didn't arrive.

The Hittite king went to war over it. He attacked Egyptian territory in Syria and marched his prisoners home.

By the Hittites' own account, the prisoners were sick. Whatever they had spread through Turkey and it didn't stop. It killed the king. It killed his heir a year later. It was still killing people twenty years on, when a younger son was on the throne, and that son wrote a series of prayers that are among the most affecting things to come down to us from this age.

He doesn't boast. He tells the gods his country is dying. The men who plowed the gods' fields are dead, he says, and the women who ground the flour for the gods' bread are dead. He's had the archives searched to find out what his family did to deserve this. And he's found it: his father broke a sworn treaty when he attacked Egypt. So he says it. "It is so. We have done it."

Follow the chain. A royal marriage, the one tool these kings trusted above all others, failed once. The failure killed a prince, started a war, and loosed a plague that ran for twenty years. The roads that carried the tin and the brides carried the disease too. Nobody had put that on the bill.

[VISUAL: Object — Tutankhamun's iron dagger with its gold sheath (Cairo).]

There's something else in that boy's tomb I've been waiting to show you, because it answers a question I asked you two hours ago.

Among the gold was a dagger with an iron blade. The iron came from a meteorite. It was one of the most precious things he owned.

So here's the question from the days of Sargon. Iron ore is in practically every range of hills. Usable tin deposits are much less evenly spread, often far from the places making bronze. Why did these people build their world on bronze?

There are two reasons. The first surprised me. Bronze was better. When you smelt iron ore in a simple furnace you don't get anything like steel. You get a spongy lump of soft metal that has to be hammered for hours, and a blade made from it is softer than a good bronze one and takes a worse edge. Making it hard means working carbon into it and quenching it, and nobody had that worked out reliably. There's a letter from a Hittite king of this period, answering another ruler who'd asked him for iron. He says there's no good iron in his storehouse at the moment, that it's a bad season for making it, and that for now he's sending one dagger blade. One.

The second reason is simpler. Bronze worked. The tin was arriving. The copper was arriving. Every smith from Greece to Babylon knew the recipe, and every king could get the ingredients as long as the ships and the caravans kept moving.

That was the trade these people made. They took the better metal, and the price was that they depended on a supply chain no one of them controlled. For three hundred years it looked like a very good trade.

[VISUAL: Map — the Orontes River in Syria; Kadesh on its mound; the four Egyptian divisions strung out on the road south. Date card: 1274 BCE.]

Several decades later, Egypt and the Hittites fought at Kadesh, a fortified city beside the Orontes River in what is now Syria. Most of the detailed battle story comes from Ramses's own inscriptions and pictures. They were made to celebrate him. We can follow that account without mistaking it for a neutral report from both armies.

The king of Egypt is Ramses the Second. He's about twenty-five and he's been on the throne five years. He marches north into Syria with four divisions, about twenty thousand men, to take a fortress city called Kadesh. The divisions are a day apart on the road, and Ramses is out in front with the first.

Near the city his men pick up two local herders, who say they've come to change sides. They tell him the Hittite king is terrified of him and is hanging back a hundred and twenty miles to the north. Ramses is delighted. He hurries ahead with one division and pitches camp beside Kadesh.

Then his patrols bring in two more prisoners. These are Hittite scouts. They're beaten until they talk, and what they say is that the two herders were plants. The Hittite king isn't a hundred and twenty miles away. He's on the other side of the city, behind the hill it's built on, with his whole army, including more chariots than anyone has ever put in the field.

Ramses sends riders to hurry up the divisions behind him. It's too late. The Hittite chariots come across the river and hit the second division while it's strung out on the march, and scatter it. Then they turn and go straight through the Egyptian camp.

What saved him was partly luck. The Hittite chariot crews got into the camp and stopped to loot it. And a separate Egyptian force that had come up by the coast road arrived at exactly that moment. Ramses rallied what he had and held on until dark.

By the next day both armies were exhausted and neither could break the other. Ramses went home. The Hittites kept Kadesh, and the country south of it as well.

[VISUAL: Photo — the Kadesh reliefs at Abu Simbel, Ramses enormous in his chariot.]

So who won? If you go by the map, the Hittites did. If you go by the walls of Egypt, Ramses did, gloriously and by himself. He had the battle carved on at least five temples. In his version his troops ran away, he prayed to Amun, and then he charged the enemy alone and drove them into the river. He's shown thirty feet tall. The Hittites are the size of mice.

He ruled for another sixty-one years and he never stopped telling that story. He's the greatest public relations man in history. He nearly got his army wiped out by believing two herders, and three thousand years later tourists stand in front of his version and take it for a victory.

About fifteen years after the battle the two kingdoms made peace, and it wasn't because they'd grown fond of each other. The Hittite king by then was the brother of the one who'd fought at Kadesh. He had taken the throne from his nephew, and the nephew had fled to Egypt. A man who's stolen a crown needs other kings to recognize him. And on the Hittites' eastern border, the Assyrian kingdom had become a formidable military rival.

So they signed a treaty. Egyptian and Hittite versions survive, preserving the same central agreement, though not identical wording. Neither will attack the other. Each will come to the other's aid. And each will send back the other's fugitives, with a clause that says the people sent back aren't to be punished or have their houses torn down. It's the oldest peace treaty between great powers that survives. There's a copy on the wall at the United Nations in New York.

The Hittite copy was sealed by two people, the king and the queen. Her name was Puduhepa and she's worth knowing. She ran the kingdom's correspondence alongside her husband and after him. When the peace was to be sealed with a marriage and Ramses complained that the bride and her dowry were slow in coming, she wrote back herself. I'll paraphrase. She asks him whether the king of Egypt owns nothing at all. She says that for him to squeeze her for more is beneath him.

Ramses gave as good as he got. When the Hittite court asked him to send an Egyptian doctor to help the king's sister have a child, he replied, in effect: I know your sister. She's fifty. No, she's sixty. Nobody can make a medicine for that. But he'd send a doctor anyway.

One of Ramses' sons spent those peaceful years looking much further back. His name was Khaemwaset, and he was high priest at Memphis, Egypt's old capital near the pyramids. On one old pyramid, an inscription credits him with preserving the name of the king who built it. By then that king had been dead for more than a thousand years. So here is an Egyptian prince looking at monuments already ancient to him, and making sure an earlier ruler's name stays attached to the stone. He made sure his own name was there too.

That's the peak. The two great powers are at peace, their rulers are teasing each other by mail, and a Hittite princess is on her way south with her escort.

[VISUAL: Reconstruction — the palace of Pylos, the archive room by the entrance. Object: a Linear B tablet with small pictograms.]

Let me show you two places at that peak, because I'm going to need you to remember them.

The first is in Greece, at a palace called Pylos, on a hill above a bay in the southwest. I should be straight about the evidence. Everything we know about this office comes from its very last year, for a reason you'll hear later. But the routine was generations old. So picture a clerk at that desk around 1250, two generations before the end. He's the Counter. There are perhaps fifty people in this kingdom who can do what he does.

His desk is by the front entrance. He writes on palm-sized pieces of damp clay in the script the Greeks borrowed from Crete, and what he writes is the palace's day. Figs and grain for thirty-seven women and their children in the weaving unit. Bronze issued to the smiths in the villages, by weight, with a note of which smiths got none this season. Sheep by the tens of thousands. Chariot wheels, serviceable and unserviceable. Rowers owed by each town on the coast.

Some groups of women are identified by place names across the Aegean, in what is now Turkey. Scholars have argued that some were captives or otherwise dependent workers. The labels do not give us one clear legal status for every woman. They show people being administered through work and rations, with very little of their own story attached.

The tablets are working records, usually written on clay and left unbaked. Many concern a short period of administration rather than a permanent history. Imagine a clerk setting down a tablet after checking a delivery. It can dry hard enough to handle without becoming fired pottery. That difference will matter when we return to this archive.

The surviving Linear B records are overwhelmingly administrative. We have no surviving body of letters or poems in this script. That doesn't let us inspect every person's abilities in the kingdom, but it does show how closely the evidence for this kind of writing is tied to palace work. Lose that work and the training of new scribes becomes much harder to sustain.

Remember the shelf.

[VISUAL: Reconstruction — the harbor of Ugarit at dusk, ships at the quay, lamps in doorways. Object: the Ugaritic alphabet tablet, thirty signs.]

The second is at Ugarit, the port in Syria, at the end of a working day. Picture one of the men on the quay.

He's one of the Carrier's men, and he's spent the day hauling those fifty-pound copper slabs up a gangplank, so his shoulder is raw in the place where his ancestor's was raw from the tow rope. The quay behind him is piled with things that don't belong together. Copper from the island. Oil jars from Greece. Bolts of Egyptian linen. A crate of blue stone that started out in Afghanistan.

Imagine several languages being spoken around him. Ugarit's scribes used more than one language and more than one writing system. Among them was an alphabet of about thirty signs, written in wedges on clay. It was used for serious scribal work, including letters, religious texts, and accounts. Keep that in mind: alphabetic writing was already useful before the palaces of this world fell.

Put a singer in a doorway as the working day ends. We cannot recover this particular woman or her song; she belongs to our imagined evening. But a hymn to the goddess Nikkal really does survive among Ugarit's tablets, with signs interpreted as musical instructions. The archives contain more than prices and royal demands.

He thinks this is how the world is. The ships come. They've always come. His grandfather worked this quay.

[VISUAL: Part card update — THE FAMILY, 1250 BCE: five countries. The Keeper writes to his "brothers"; the Counter keeps a palace's accounts; the Carrier sails the circuit; the Maker waits for tin; the Farmer feeds all of them.]

So here's the family at the top.

The Keeper is a Great King who calls other Great Kings his brother, and marries their daughters, and has never met one of them.

The Counter is a clerk in a palace, writing in a language that isn't his own or keeping accounts nobody else can read.

The Carrier sails the circuit with ten tons of somebody else's copper.

The Maker can't work unless a ship comes in from Cyprus and a caravan comes in from the end of the earth, both in the same season.

And the Farmer feeds all four, and his sister is on a list.

This world offers possibilities the first household never had: specialized crafts, distant markets, ambitious literature, goods from beyond the horizon. The gains are not shared equally. A powerful ruler, an independent merchant, and a dependent weaver occupy very different positions. What connects them is the work that has to keep being done: harvests, shipments, agreements, and payments.

The people inside it knew about bad harvests, hostile neighbors, and failed deliveries. Their letters say so. What they could not know was which troubles would be overcome, as many had been before, and which institutions would not survive the next generations.

The rain is about to change.
