## The Draft

---

[VISUAL: Map — the Tigris River in northern Iraq, a marching column moving north. Date card: 401 BCE.]

In the year 401 BCE, a Greek soldier named Xenophon walked past a wall he couldn't explain.

He was having a bad year. He was one of ten thousand Greek mercenaries who had hired on with a Persian prince, marched a thousand miles into the Persian Empire to help him steal the throne, and watched him get killed in the battle that was supposed to win it. Then their own generals went to a peace dinner and were murdered at the table. So here were ten thousand Greeks in the middle of what is now Iraq, with no employer and no commanders, walking home through people who wanted them dead.

Xenophon took notes anyway. He was that kind of man. And somewhere along the Tigris River, he wrote down that the army passed a deserted city with a wall around it a hundred feet high and twenty-five feet thick.

[VISUAL: Scale diagram — a 100-foot wall beside a ten-story building and a human figure.]

A hundred feet is a ten-story building. This one was made of brick, by hand, and it ran for miles, and there was nobody living inside it. No smoke, no market noise. Pigeons.

He asked the locals who had built it. They said the Medes, an old people from the mountains. A day's march later the army passed a second ruin, bigger than the first, with a base of polished stone full of fossil shells. Same question. Roughly the same answer. Somebody added that Zeus had frightened the inhabitants with thunder and that was how the place fell. Nobody seemed bothered about the details. Xenophon wrote it all down and kept walking, because he had other problems.

He had just walked past Nineveh.

[VISUAL: Map — Nineveh and Nimrud marked; the Assyrian Empire at its height shaded from Iran to Egypt.]

Nineveh had been the capital of the Assyrian Empire, which at its height ran from the mountains of Iran to the Nile and taxed everything in between. It was the biggest city on Earth in its day. The Bible calls it "that great city" and says it took three days to cross. And it had been burned to the ground, by a coalition of its enemies, two hundred and eleven years before Xenophon showed up.

Two hundred and eleven years. From us, that's back to about 1815. Napoleon. We know quite a lot about Napoleon. But the people farming in the shadow of those walls could not tell a curious Greek the name of the city, or the name of the empire, or get the builders right. In two centuries the greatest power the world had ever seen had turned into a shrug and a story about thunder.

I think that's one of the most frightening facts in history, and it's where I want to start, because this isn't a series about Assyria.

It's about the world that was lost before that one.

Assyria was an heir. The cities, the writing, the laws, the idea that one king could rule a hundred peoples, all of that was already ancient when Nineveh was built. It went back more than two thousand years further, to the first cities anyone ever lived in, and it ran forward from there to a few centuries of something that looks strangely like our own world: kings writing each other letters, ships carrying goods from three continents in one hold, a credit system, customer complaints, a peace treaty with an extradition clause. We call that long stretch the Bronze Age. Around 1200 BCE most of it came apart, inside one lifetime, and the people who came afterward forgot it so thoroughly that when the Greeks of Xenophon's day told stories about it, they thought they were telling stories about gods and giants.

[VISUAL: Timeline — 3500 BCE first cities; 1200 BCE collapse; 401 BCE Xenophon; today. Bronze Age span highlighted.]

So here's the question I'd like you to carry through the whole series. How do people build a world that connected, live in it for centuries, lean on it until it feels like the weather, and then lose it so completely that their own descendants stand on the ruins and guess?

I'm going to tell it in a particular way, and I should explain two things before we start.

The first is the questions. I like simple ones. Why would anybody start farming? Why is every early city on a river? Why does an hour have sixty minutes? How do you mail a letter before there's paper? If iron is in every hillside, why did they bother with bronze? Most history skips those because the answers are supposed to be obvious. They aren't, and the real answers are usually better than the dates.

The second is the family.

Four thousand years is too long to follow any real person, and the Bronze Age has more kings with unpronounceable names than anyone should be asked to remember. So I'm going to cheat, and I'm telling you now so you know it's a cheat. We're going to start in one house, about seven thousand years ago, with five people in it. One of them works the field. One of them minds the grain. One of them is good at counting. One of them likes to walk to far places and come back with things. One of them is clever with tools.

In that first house they're one family, and every one of them does a little of everything.

As the world changes, their jobs are going to change, and I'm going to follow the jobs. The one who minds the grain will end up a king. The one who counts will invent writing without meaning to. The walker becomes a merchant, the tool-maker becomes a smith, and the one in the field stays in the field for four thousand years and pays for all of it. They're not real people. They're a way of keeping track. But at nearly every step there's a real person standing where one of them would be standing, with a real name and sometimes real mail, and whenever that happens I'll tell you, and that person you can trust.

Watch what happens to the five of them. By the middle of this story they'll live in five different countries, they'll never meet, and not one of them will be able to eat dinner without the other four. I think that's the whole secret of the Bronze Age. It's how they got rich, and it's what killed them.

When the evidence runs out I'll say so, once, and move on. There are things nobody knows, and some of them are the best parts.

[VISUAL: Fade from the ruined wall to darkness; a single ember.]

We begin a long time before the wall Xenophon couldn't explain. Before there was a city anywhere, or a king, or a single written word. In the dark, in a small house that smells of smoke and sheep, where someone is about to wake up.
