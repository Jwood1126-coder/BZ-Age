[VISUAL: Part card — WHEN: 2334 BCE. WHERE: central Iraq, where the two rivers come closest together. THE FAMILY: the Keeper is about to get ambitious.]

You know this story.

A woman has a baby she can't keep. She puts him in a basket made of reeds, seals it with tar so it floats, and sets it on the river. The river carries him away. Someone downstream finds him and raises him. He grows up to lead his people.

That's Moses. It's also a king named Sargon, and Sargon's version is set about a thousand years earlier. A legend written long after his death puts these words in his mouth: "My mother was a high priestess. My father I never knew. She set me in a basket of rushes and sealed the lid with tar. She cast me into the river, and the river did not drown me." A man drawing water fishes him out and raises him as a gardener, and then the goddess of love and war takes a liking to him, and he becomes king.

The surviving copies of Sargon's birth legend are from the first millennium BCE, long after the historical king. The setting of a story and the date someone wrote it down are different things. We can compare its floating basket with the Moses tradition without pretending we have a contemporary record of Sargon's infancy.

And I think there's a tell in his name. Sargon isn't a name your mother gives you. In his language it means "the king is legitimate." A man doesn't usually call himself the True King unless somebody has doubts.

[VISUAL: Map — the cities of the southern plain as separate dots; one dot in the north, Kish, highlighted.]

Here's what we can piece together. We're on the same river plain as last time, in what's now Iraq, around 2350 BCE. Uruk is nine hundred years old. The Great Pyramid in Egypt has been standing for two centuries. And the plain is still a dozen separate cities, each with its farmland, its god and its king, fighting each other over water. Sargon turns up as a servant to the king of one of them, a city called Kish. He's the cupbearer, the man who hands the king his drink, which sounds like a waiter and is closer to chief of staff. Somehow, and nobody recorded how, the king is gone and Sargon is in charge.

Then he does something on a scale nobody in this country has managed. He marches south and takes the other cities, one after another. His inscriptions claim thirty-four battles. He beats the strongest king in the south, a man who had just bullied most of the other cities into line himself. He puts him in a wooden collar like a dog, and parades him to the gate of the chief god's temple. He keeps going until he reaches the Persian Gulf, and there, his inscription says, he washed his weapons in the sea.

Then he turns around and goes the other way, north and west, up the Euphrates, across Syria, to the mountains by the Mediterranean.

[VISUAL: Map — Sargon's conquests spreading from the Gulf to the Mediterranean; overlay: "about 1,000 miles end to end."]

End to end it's about a thousand miles. On a modern map it's most of Iraq, a slice of Iran, eastern Syria, and the edge of Turkey and Lebanon. Egypt already had a kingdom stretching along the Nile. Here in Mesopotamia, rulers had controlled more than one city before, but Sargon's conquests brought a much larger territory under one center.

He ruled it from a city called Akkad, not far from where Baghdad is now. I can't show you a picture. Akkad has never been found. It's somewhere under the mud, the capital of Mesopotamia's first lasting empire, and nobody knows where.

So, first question. What's an empire? Here I mean one ruling power holding other peoples and territories under its authority. How is that different from a strong city that beats up its neighbors?

The cities of the plain had been fighting each other for five hundred years. When you won, you took some loot and some land, maybe some hostages, and you went home. The loser kept his own king, his own god, his own storehouse.

Sargon's conquests became a sustained project of ruling distant cities. His inscriptions describe appointed governors, military victories, and resources gathered toward Akkad. Earlier rulers had already imposed authority beyond their own city walls. The change here is the scale and duration of the effort, not the invention of conquest in a single man's lifetime.

[VISUAL: Illustration — a village on the plain; a governor's party arriving by boat.]

And I want you to see how that looked from the bottom, because it's stranger than the map.

Go to the Farmer. He's in a village outside Ur, in the far south. His house is the same house. Mud brick, dirt floor, grain jars. His tools are the tools his grandfather used. The god in the temple down the road is the god who has always been there.

One thing has changed. It's the man who comes for the barley.

It used to be a man from Ur. He spoke the Farmer's language, Sumerian, and he worked for the king of Ur, who served the god of Ur, and that was simply how the world was arranged. Now there's a governor who was sent from somewhere five weeks upriver, by a king the Farmer will never lay eyes on. The governor speaks a different language at home, one called Akkadian, which is no relation at all to Sumerian. It's a distant cousin of Hebrew and Arabic. The barley still goes onto the barge. But it goes further now, to feed people the Farmer has never heard of.

Picture what that change asks of a household. The grain must still be threshed, measured, and carried out. Someone has to argue about the amount, find labor for the load, and decide what remains for the family. The new ruler can be remote while the demand is close enough to put a hand into the grain jar.

A ruler from outside wasn't an unimaginable novelty. But for this household, authority now reaches much farther beyond the familiar city and its fields.

Second question. How do you run a place you've never seen?

Sargon's officials could draw on generations of city government. Now they had to adapt that experience to a much larger territory, with more languages, longer journeys, and more places that might refuse an order.

How do you support that authority? One of Sargon's inscriptions says that five thousand four hundred men ate bread in front of him every day. Scholars have read that as a body of troops, or as a broader royal household. It does not give us a secure head count for the world's first standing army. But either reading brings us back to the storehouse: a king boasting about power is also boasting about how many people he can feed.

How do you make the accounts from one province mean something in another? You give everyone the same measures. A later king of this family issued a standard basket for the whole empire, so that a measure of barley was the same in the north as in the south.

How do you talk to all these people? You pick one language for business. That was Akkadian, written with the same wedge-shaped marks the Counter's line had invented for Sumerian. The Counter now works in two languages and serves a king he's never met.

And how do you get people who worship different gods to accept you? For that, Sargon used his daughter.

[VISUAL: Object — the Disk of Enheduanna (Penn Museum), the carved figure in the tiered robe highlighted. Map: Ur.]

Her name is Enheduanna, and she's the person I want you to follow through the first half of this part, because she lived to see this empire through four reigns.

Ur, down in the south, was one of the holiest cities in the old country. Its temple belonged to the moon god. The most important religious office in that temple was the high priestess, and whoever held it was, in the theology, the god's wife on earth. Sargon made his daughter high priestess of the moon at Ur.

You can see what he was doing. The southerners had their own gods and their own priests, and those priests had every reason to hate him. So he puts his own child at the top of their most sacred temple. The message is that the new king isn't replacing your god. Your god has accepted the new king's family. It also puts a person he trusts in a city five weeks from his capital.

We know what she looked like, roughly. In the 1920s the same Leonard Woolley who dug the royal tombs found a stone disc in the ruins of the priestess's house at Ur. It had been smashed in ancient times and he pieced it together. It shows a woman in a long tiered robe overseeing an offering, and the inscription on the back names her: Enheduanna, wife of the moon god, daughter of Sargon.

But the disc isn't why she matters. She's remembered as a writer.

Under Enheduanna's name we have fierce, personal hymns to the goddess Inanna. They survive in copies made centuries after her lifetime, and scholars disagree about how much of the poetry goes back to her. Her historical position is secure; the relationship between that woman and every line attributed to her is harder to establish. The works made her name part of a literary tradition, not just a royal family tree.

Notice whose daughter she is. The Counter's tool, the thing invented to track barley, has just been picked up by the Keeper's family and used to say how a person feels. Nobody planned that either.

[VISUAL: Object — cylinder seal of Shu-ilishu (Louvre), with its inscription "interpreter of Meluhha."]

Meanwhile the empire is doing what empires do, which is pull things toward the middle.

Sargon boasted that ships from distant lands reached his capital. One trading partner was Meluhha, generally identified with the Indus civilization, far to the east. There is a later seal, from around the end of the third millennium BCE, belonging to a man called Shu-ilishu. Its inscription calls him an interpreter of Meluhha. It doesn't put him at Sargon's own dock. It gives us something more modest and wonderfully human: someone whose job was to make that distant trade understandable.

Someone spoke both sides of a conversation that we can now recover from only one side. To him it was work.

That's the Carrier's world now. His line isn't hauling barges for one temple anymore. They're moving goods between civilizations.

And what they're moving, more than anything, is metal. By now more and more of the smiths are using the tin recipe in place of the arsenic one. Nine parts copper, one part tin: bronze. It's harder, it casts better, and it doesn't cripple the Maker. But there's no tin in Iraq. There's none anywhere near. It comes from mountains far to the east or north, at the far end of a chain of middlemen. The best weapons in that standing army depend on a road that begins somewhere no one in the empire has ever been.

Which raises a question I'd like you to keep at the back of your mind for a couple of hours. Iron ore is everywhere. It's in practically every range of hills from Spain to India. Nobody has to haul it a thousand miles. So why are these people building their whole world on a metal that needs a rare ingredient from the edge of the map? Why is this the Bronze Age? I'll answer it when we get to the top of that world. Just notice that it's odd.

[VISUAL: Object — the Victory Stele of Naram-Sin (Louvre); zoom to the horned helmet. Date card: 2254 BCE.]

Sargon dies after about fifty years on the throne. Two of his sons rule after him and hold it together. Then his grandson takes over, and the grandson's name is Naram-Sin.

Naram-Sin is the first ruler in this story who was born into an empire. He never knew a world without it, and he was good at the job. He pushed the borders further than his grandfather had.

Then nearly everything rose against him at once. The cities of the south and the cities of the north both revolted, with their own kings, in the same year. By his own account he fought nine battles in that one year and won every one.

And here is what happened next, as he had it inscribed on the base of a copper statue that was found by a road crew in northern Iraq in 1975. After the nine victories, it says, the people of his city asked the gods to make him the god of their city. And the gods agreed. And they built him a temple.

From then on he writes his name with the sign that marks a god. On his great victory monument, which is in Paris now, he's shown climbing a mountain over the bodies of his enemies, twice the size of his own soldiers, wearing a helmet with horns on it. Horns were what gods wore.

Third question. Can a living man be a god?

In Egypt, yes, and we'll get there in a minute. In Iraq it was new, and it was shocking. Their own tradition was strict about it. They kept a list of every king since the beginning of time, and it starts with the line "When kingship was lowered from heaven." Kingship was a thing the gods lent out, to one city at a time. The early kings on the list reign for tens of thousands of years apiece, but even they are borrowers. The king serves the god. He isn't one.

Naram-Sin had just said the rule didn't apply to him.

The poetry attributed to Enheduanna also tells a story of political violence at Ur. Its speaker says a rebel has forced her from her religious office. We cannot turn all its details into a securely dated eyewitness account of Naram-Sin's revolt, but we can hear how it presents the experience of losing power.

The voice in the poem is no longer simply a royal daughter occupying an important office. She is someone pleading for that position and her good name to be restored. The institution that made her powerful has also made her vulnerable.

One of those poems tells the story. In it she says the rebel has driven her out of the temple, that she's been made to fly like a swallow from the window. She says "my good name is destroyed." And then, in the middle of a hymn to a goddess, the speaker names herself. She says: "I am Enheduanna. Let me speak to you."

The poem presents a restoration of her position. That is the story the work tells; the political sequence behind it is less certain.

What holds my attention is the combination: a real royal daughter in a powerful religious office, and a later literary tradition that speaks in her name with extraordinary force. We don't need to know every stage of that tradition to hear a person rather than another total of barley.

That's the last we see of her. The rest of this part happens after her time.

[VISUAL: Text card — "The Curse of Akkad." Then map: the empire shrinking back to a single city.]

Fourth question, and it's the one this series is really about. Why do empires fall?

Within a couple of generations of Naram-Sin, Akkad was gone. His son held on. After that it was four kings in three years, and then a mountain people called the Gutians were raiding the plain and the cities were on their own again.

About a century later, somebody wrote down why. It's a poem called the Curse of Akkad, and its explanation is simple. Naram-Sin got above himself. He wanted to rebuild the temple of the chief god and the omens said no. He waited seven years. Then he lost patience, marched on the temple, and wrecked it. The gods were furious, and they sent the mountain people down on the plain as a punishment. The poem ends with a curse: may your canals fill with silt, may grass grow on your towpaths.

As history it doesn't hold up. The empire outlasted Naram-Sin by a generation. But I want you to notice the shape of it, because you already know it. The great man reaches too far, and the gods bring him down. Fifteen hundred years later the Greeks built their tragedies on that. The Hebrew prophets used it. You use it. The next time some big institution collapses and your first thought is to look for the arrogant man at the top, you're repeating a story that priests in Iraq pressed into clay four thousand years ago, about a king who wore horns.

So what did happen?

There are physical clues to environmental stress around this period: dust in marine sediments, changes in settlement, and records preserved in caves and lakes. Some point to severe drying in parts of the region around 2200 BCE. They do not give us a single weather report for every province, and the timing of the Akkadian state's political decline does not fit every climatic record neatly.

Drought may have made existing conflicts and provisioning problems harder to manage. That is a plausible connection, not a complete explanation recovered from a ring of dust. Different districts could face different pressures, and the documents and the ground do not let us isolate one cause of the empire's end.

Remember the possibility: a difficult harvest can be harder to survive when conflict and disrupted supplies are already wearing down a state's resources.

Now a side trip to Egypt, where a different state faced its own period of fragmentation. The comparison is useful, but we should not assume that similar-looking endings prove a single simultaneous cause.

[VISUAL: Map — pull west from Iraq to Egypt. Photo: the Giza pyramids. Date card: "Side trip — Egypt, 2560 BCE."]

Egypt had been a single kingdom for seven hundred years by now. Its king wasn't the steward of a god. He was one, from the day he was crowned, and nobody found that shocking. And by Sargon's day the Egyptians had already built the thing they're known for. The Great Pyramid went up around 2560 BCE. When Sargon was born it had already been standing for about two hundred years.

Here's a simple question about it. Who built it? Everyone has heard it was slaves in chains. It wasn't that.

In 2013, at an ancient harbor on the Red Sea, archaeologists found a bundle of papyrus scrolls. They're the oldest written papyri ever discovered, and they turned out to be a logbook. It was kept by a man named Merer, who was the foreman of a crew of about forty, and his crew's job was to haul limestone for the Great Pyramid.

It's a wonderfully dull document. Day after day: we loaded stone at the quarry. We sailed downriver. We spent the night at such-and-such. We unloaded at the building site. We went back. He records that the crew was paid in bread and beer. It's a working man's diary from inside the construction of the Great Pyramid, and what it shows is organized crews doing a season's work.

And next to the pyramids, archaeologists found the town the builders lived in. It had bakeries turning out bread on an industrial scale. The bone dumps show that the workers ate beef, a great deal of it. Their skeletons have broken bones that were set and healed, which means somebody was giving them medical care. They were organized in gangs, and the gangs scratched their names on the blocks. One was called the Friends of Khufu, after the king. Another was the Drunkards of Menkaure.

So that's the Farmer's kind of work in Egypt. He's called up for a season, and I doubt he had much say in it. He's fed well, he hauls stone for a god-king alongside men from his own village, and they give their team a silly name.

Now put Egypt beside Akkad. Late in the third millennium BCE, the kingdom that had built the great pyramids broke into competing regional powers. Poor Nile floods may have added to its troubles. One provincial governor boasted in his tomb that he had kept his district fed while other people starved. That is a ruler telling posterity why he deserves to be remembered, not a survey of the whole country. But it tells us what he wanted credit for: keeping food available when other authorities could not.

The Egyptian and Iraqi disruptions belong to the same broad period. Some chronologies bring them close together, but the dates do not give us two synchronized disasters. I think the comparison is worth making anyway. Environmental pressure could make it harder for either government to hold its provinces together. Whether the same climatic change helped weaken both remains an open question.

Egypt eventually reunited. For now, come back to Iraq.

[VISUAL: Photo — the Ziggurat of Ur today. Date card: 2112 BCE.]

Akkad was gone, but the idea wasn't. Once somebody has ruled all the cities, every ambitious king knows it can be done.

It took about a hundred years. Around 2112 BCE a king of Ur, Enheduanna's old city, pulled the south back together. He built the great stepped temple tower at Ur, which is still standing, about two hundred miles south of Baghdad. He issued the oldest set of laws we have, about three centuries before Hammurabi, the Babylonian king whose laws we will meet next, and in his laws you mostly pay fines.

His son ruled for forty-eight years and I can't resist telling you about him. He claimed, in a hymn he had written about himself, that he once ran from one holy city to another and back in a single day, through a hailstorm, to celebrate a festival in both. That's about two hundred miles. He also claimed to be the best scribe in the land. And he, too, declared himself a god. Naram-Sin's idea, the one that was supposed to have doomed Akkad, had stuck.

What this kingdom was better at than anyone before it was paperwork. More than a hundred thousand clay tablets survive from about fifty years of its government. It's one of the best-documented half centuries in all of ancient history, and nearly all of it is receipts.

Listen to what they tracked. In one province, the state weaving sheds employed about fifteen thousand women. Each was owed a fixed amount of barley a month, so much oil a year, so much wool. When a weaver died, they wrote it down and stopped the ration. When a sheep died, the shepherd had to turn in the skin to prove he hadn't sold it.

The women doing the Farmer's sister's kind of work appear in these administrative records through obligations, rations, and labor. Sometimes ancient records do preserve individual names. What we usually lack is an account in the woman's own voice of the life behind the entry.

It lasted about a century.

[VISUAL: Map — Ur squeezed from three sides: herders from the west, a breakaway official in the north, Elam from the east. Date card: 2004 BCE.]

The trouble came from three directions.

From the west, herders. They were called Amorites, and they'd been drifting in from the Syrian steppe for generations, drawn by the green fields. One king of Ur built a wall right across the country between the two rivers to stop them, a hundred and something miles long. The wall had a name, and the name tells you everything about how they felt. They called it Keeps the Nomads Away.

It didn't keep the nomads away.

From inside, a betrayal. Under the last king of Ur, the harvests failed and the price of grain in the capital went up sixty times over. The king sent one of his senior officials north with a boatload of silver to buy grain. The official bought it. Then he wrote back that the roads were too dangerous to ship it, and he'd need to be made governor of the city he was in to keep it safe. And then of the next city. Within a few years he was a king, and Ur's grain was his.

The exchange survives in later copies, part of the literary memory of Ur's fall. It presents a king unable to make an official deliver urgently needed grain. Whether every accusation is reliable or not, the practical problem is clear: commanding a shipment and getting it to the city are different things.

The enemy showed up anyway. In 2004 BCE an army from Elam, the kingdom in the hills of southwestern Iran, came down and sacked Ur. They looted the temple of the moon, where Enheduanna had lived. They took the last king away in chains, and he died in Iran.

And afterward someone at Ur wrote a poem. It's called the Lament for Ur, and it's four hundred lines of grief. The goddess of the city walks through the ruins weeping. The gates are broken. The dead lie in the streets where the festivals used to be. Storms have blown the city away, she says, and she has nowhere to live.

They'd invented a kind of poem for the death of a city. They would need it again.

[VISUAL: Part card update — THE FAMILY, 2000 BCE: the Keeper has learned empire; the Counter writes poetry and law in two languages; the Carrier sails to India; the Maker casts tin-bronze; the Farmer hands his barley to strangers.]

One more thing was dying in these centuries. People were giving up speaking Sumerian at home. We can't put a date on it. The language of the first cities, of the first writing, of Enheduanna's hymns, stopped being anyone's mother tongue. It lived on in the schools. For the next two thousand years every educated person in Iraq had to learn it, the way educated Europeans once had to learn Latin, and it was no one's native speech.

So take stock of the family at the year 2000.

The Keeper's line has ruled an empire twice and lost it twice, and has learned to call itself divine. That idea can't be unlearned.

The Counter's line can now write a poem, a law, a letter and a lie, in two languages, one of them dead.

The Carrier's line has been to India.

The Maker's line is casting bronze, and can't make it without tin from the end of the earth.

And the Farmer has learned that the man who takes his barley can change overnight. What changes for him is how far it travels, and whether anything comes back in a bad year.

For the next couple of centuries nobody holds the whole plain. There are rival kingdoms, and merchants, and new peoples moving in. And in that gap, for the first time, we get to read ordinary people's mail.
