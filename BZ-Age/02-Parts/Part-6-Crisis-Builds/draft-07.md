# Part 6 — The Slow Part
## SEVENTH DRAFT — v0.7
**Date:** November 16, 2026
**Status:** Rewritten to the draft-07 brief. Opens on the grain letter, makes one announced step back to explain the system's confidence (Mitanni), then runs forward from the treaty of 1259 to about 1200. Title changed from "The Crisis Builds".
**Driving questions:** How do you feed an empire when the rain stops? Who were the Sea Peoples? Could they have seen it coming? How does bad news travel?

## The Draft

---

[VISUAL: Part card — WHEN: about 1220 BCE, forty years after the peace treaty. WHERE: Hattusa, the Hittite capital, in central Turkey. THE FAMILY: the Keeper is short of grain.]

Sometime around the year 1220 BCE a letter went out from the court of the Hittite king to the port of Ugarit, on the Syrian coast.

It's an order. Ugarit is to provide a ship and a crew, at once, to carry two thousand measures of grain up the coast to a Hittite harbor. That's hundreds of tons. And the letter ends with a line that you don't expect in government mail. It says: this is a matter of life or death.

Think about who's writing. This is Hatti, the land of a thousand gods. Fifty years earlier its chariots nearly destroyed the king of Egypt at Kadesh. It's one of the two strongest states on Earth, and it's telling a harbor town that if a boat doesn't come, people are going to die.

And the Egyptians knew. Around the same time the king of Egypt, who was the son of Ramses the Second, had it carved on a temple wall that he'd sent grain in ships "to keep alive the land of Hatti." Egypt is keeping its old enemy from starving, and bragging about it.

So here's the first question. How does the greatest land power of the age end up begging for a boat?

To answer it I have to explain two things: what was going wrong, and why nobody was worried. I'll take the second one first, and it means going back, once.

[VISUAL: Map — Mitanni, the horse kingdom, between Hatti and Assyria; then the same map with Mitanni divided and gone. Date card: 1340s to 1260s BCE.]

Remember the table of great kings, and the horse kingdom in northern Syria. Mitanni. Its king wrote those letters to Egypt complaining about gold-plated statues. For a hundred years it was one of the four or five powers that ran the world.

Then it was taken apart. The Hittite king, the same one who got the letter from the Egyptian widow, beat it in war around 1340. The king of the horse kingdom was murdered by one of his own sons. The Hittites took the western half and made it a puppet. And the eastern half was swallowed over the next two generations by its own former subject, Assyria, the merchant city.

By the time of the peace treaty the horse kingdom didn't exist. One of the pillars of the world had been removed. We aren't even sure where its capital was.

And nothing happened. The trade routes bent around the gap. Assyria took the empty chair at the table. The Hittite king grumbled about having to call an upstart "brother," and then did. The tin kept coming. The princesses kept travelling. Every court in the world had watched a great power vanish, and what every court learned was that the system could take it.

That lesson is the one I think about most in this whole story, because it was true and it was the wrong lesson. The horse kingdom's fall was survivable because everything around it was healthy. The routes could shift because there were strong kingdoms to shift through. What nobody had tested was what happens when the parts fail together.

So the world of 1259 BCE, the world of the peace treaty, was confident. It had been tested and it had passed.

Now forward, and what was going wrong.

[VISUAL: Photo — a cross-section of ancient juniper timber with its rings; three very narrow rings highlighted. Text: 1198, 1197, 1196 BCE.]

It starts with the weather, and we know about the weather because trees keep a diary.

A tree lays down one ring a year. In a good year the ring is wide. In a dry year it's narrow. Centuries after this story ends, a people in central Turkey built a huge burial mound for one of their kings and lined the chamber with whole juniper logs, and those logs were already ancient when they were cut. The rings in them run right through the years we're talking about. A team published the measurements in 2023.

They show the Hittite heartland getting drier from about 1250 BCE on. Bad years came more often. And then there's a run of three rings so thin they're hard to see. Three years in a row of drought as bad as anything in the whole record. We can put dates on them: 1198, 1197 and 1196 BCE.

That isn't the only evidence. Mud from the bottom of the Sea of Galilee and from a salt lake in Cyprus holds pollen, and the pollen shows the same thing from the other side: the trees and crops thinning out and the dry-country scrub moving in, across about a century and a half.

Now go to the Farmer, on the Turkish plateau. He doesn't have a river like the Nile that floods no matter what. He farms on rain. And every farming family since the first house has known one rule. You never eat the seed.

There's a jar in the corner with next year's planting in it. In a bad year you eat less. You eat the animals. You eat things you'd be ashamed to serve. The jar stays shut. Then there's a second bad year, and the animals are already gone. The tax collector comes anyway, because his tablet doesn't have a column for rainfall. And in the third year the family is sitting in front of the jar.

Eat it, and you live until spring and have nothing to plant. Don't, and you may not see spring. I'm imagining that scene, but only just. That's what three thin tree rings mean when you turn them into people. It was happening in a hundred thousand houses.

Second question, then. How do you feed an empire when the rain stops?

For a while, out of the bins. The Keeper has been storing grain for six thousand years and by now he's good at it. At the Hittite capital archaeologists found underground silos the size of swimming pools, sealed with clay, that could hold enough to feed the whole city for a year or more. That's what a state is for, in the end. It's your grandmother's grain bin, big enough to carry a country through a bad harvest.

It can carry you through one. Maybe two. But a store only helps if good years come along to fill it again. If they don't, you're spending savings, and from the outside a household spending its savings looks exactly like a household doing fine. The house is the same, the meals are the same. Then one month the account is empty.

When the bins ran low, they bought. That's the letter. Grain from Egypt, where the Nile was still rising, shipped up the coast and through the port of Ugarit and on to Turkey. A capital in the middle of the highlands, days from any sea, was now eating by boat.

And there was a third group of mouths that made it worse. For centuries the Hittite kings had fought their wars for people as much as for land. Their own records count the captives along with the cattle: fifteen thousand from one campaign, marched back to the plateau and settled on royal estates to work the fields. That's the Farmer's sister again, and her brothers. So a good share of the people now going hungry in central Turkey had been brought there in chains to grow the food that wasn't growing.

[VISUAL: Object — the treaty tablet of the Hittite king with the ruler of Amurru; highlight the line where a king's name has been erased.]

Now watch what the kings do under pressure, because they did something you'll recognize.

By the 1230s the Hittites' great worry was Assyria. The merchant city had become a military power, it had taken the old horse kingdom's land, and it had just beaten a Hittite army in the field.

So the Hittite king wrote a treaty with one of his subject kings on the Syrian coast, and one clause in it reads like this. No merchant of yours is to go to Assyria. No merchant of Assyria is to enter your land or pass through it. And no ship of the Greeks is to be let through to him.

That's a trade embargo. It's sanctions, around 1230 BCE: you can't beat him in battle, so you try to cut off his business.

There's something else on that tablet that I find hard to look away from. Early in the text there's a list of the kings the Hittite ruler counts as his equals. The king of Egypt. The king of Babylon. The king of Assyria. And then a fourth name was written, the king of the Greeks, and the scribe went back and scratched it out. You can see the erasure.

We don't know why. Maybe the Greeks had just stopped mattering. Maybe someone decided it was an insult to include them. But there it is: one of the powers at the table, crossed off by hand, in the wet clay.

The Greeks, by the way, had been making trouble for the Hittites on the west coast of Turkey for a century. There are letters about it. A lot of the trouble centered on one town up near the straits that the Hittites called Wilusa. The Greeks had another way of saying that name. Keep it in mind until the last part.

[VISUAL: Relief — Sherden warriors with horned helmets and round shields (Abu Simbel or Medinet Habu). Map: arrows along the coasts of Turkey, Cyprus and the Levant.]

And there was trouble at sea.

Third question. Who were the Sea Peoples?

You've probably heard of them, since they're usually cast as the villains of this story: a wave of mysterious invaders who came out of nowhere and burned civilization down. I don't think that's what happened, and here's the detail that changed my mind.

Go back to Kadesh. In the pictures Ramses had carved, there are soldiers standing right next to the king with round shields and helmets with horns on them. They're his bodyguard, and they're foreigners. The Egyptians called them Sherden. They'd come raiding the Egyptian coast early in his reign, he'd beaten them, and he'd signed up the survivors.

Fifty years later the Sherden are on the list of sea raiders attacking Egypt.

So these weren't strangers. They were people who'd been on the edges of this world the whole time, as pirates when that paid and as hired soldiers when that paid better. "Sea Peoples" is a label a French scholar made up in the 1800s. The Egyptians never used it. They named separate groups: the Sherden, and half a dozen others. One group they called the Peleset. Those are almost certainly the people the Bible calls Philistines. Goliath's people.

What changed toward the end of the 1200s is that there were more of them, in bigger groups, and they weren't going home. In 1208 the king of Egypt, the one who'd sent grain to the Hittites, fought off an invasion from the western desert. The invaders had sea raiders with them as allies, and they'd brought their wives, their children and their cattle.

People don't bring cattle on a raid. They bring them when they're moving house.

I think the simplest way to see it is this. The same drought that was emptying the Hittite grain bins was hitting every coast and island around the eastern Mediterranean. Farms failed. People who couldn't eat where they were got into boats. Some went looking for work, some for land, some for a town to rob, and probably the same men did all three in different years. They weren't the cause of what's coming. They were what it looked like when it reached the sea.

A footnote on that Egyptian victory. The king had a monument put up to celebrate, and at the end of it there's a list of places he says he's crushed. One line reads: "Israel is laid waste, his seed is not." It's the first time that name appears anywhere in writing, in 1208 BCE, as a boast by a king who claimed to have wiped them out. Remember that when we get to the hills, two parts from now.

[VISUAL: Illustration — a storeroom in the Hittite capital: rows of sealed jars, a clerk with a tablet, a lamp.]

Now the question I most want to ask, and you can ask it about any collapse, including the ones people are afraid of today. Could they have seen it coming?

I don't mean, did they. I mean, was it possible? Was there enough information around that a clear-headed person at one of those courts in 1230 BCE could have stood up and said: this isn't a run of bad luck, this is the end?

I don't think so, and it wasn't because they were stupid.

They saw things. Poor harvests, raiders, a late shipment, a letter that never got an answer. Every one of those had happened before, in their fathers' time, and the world had come right. What they couldn't see was the whole of it, the entire arrangement of grain and tin and treaties and ships that I've spent three hours describing to you. Nobody had ever stood outside it. There wasn't a word for it. It's hard to notice that something is failing when you don't know it's a thing.

And the system was hiding the damage. That's the cruel part. Every grain bin and every treaty was built to absorb shocks. So each shock got absorbed, and from the palace everything looked normal, right up until the stores were gone.

So if we go looking for the person who noticed, we don't find a prophet. We find an accountant.

He's the Counter, and he's in a storeroom in the Hittite capital, and the numbers are wrong. He's counted the jars three times this month because he didn't trust the first two counts. The third came out the same. Grain doesn't care how many times you count it. The stores are a fifth below where they should be for this time of year, and it's the third year running.

He writes it down. He sends it up. He gets letters back from other storerooms saying the same thing. The provinces are sending less, because they've had poor harvests too. The grain from Egypt, which used to be a little extra, isn't extra any more.

He doesn't have a word for what he's looking at. He has "bad year" and "worse year." He's solving this month's problem with the tools he's always used.

Fourth question. How does bad news travel in a world like this?

It travels the way the goods do. And I think the first people who knew something was deeply wrong weren't kings or clerks. They were the Carrier's people: the ship owners, the caravan men, the agents in foreign ports. To a king, a trade route is a line on a list. To a merchant it's a set of people. The partner in the next port. The customs man at the river crossing. The buyer who takes the wool every autumn.

A king finds out a route has died when the tin doesn't arrive. The merchant found out months earlier, when the partner stopped answering.

I can't prove that from any one document. But there's a letter from these years, found in the house of one of the big merchants of Ugarit, written by one of his agents in a town far inland. I'll paraphrase it. There is famine in your house here, the agent says. We are all going to starve. If you don't get here soon, you won't find one of us alive.

That's the nervous system of the Bronze Age, carrying pain.

And a connected world doesn't come apart by decree. It comes apart when ten thousand sensible people each decide, one at a time, that a trip their fathers made every year isn't worth the risk this year.

[VISUAL: Photo — the walls of Tiryns, a person standing beside the stones for scale. Map: Mycenaean palaces — Mycenae, Tiryns, Pylos.]

Cross the sea to Greece, and you can measure the fear with a tape.

The Greek palaces had always had walls. In the last decades of the 1200s they rebuilt them on a different scale. At a palace called Tiryns the new walls were twenty-five feet thick, made of blocks that weigh several tons each, fitted together with no mortar. At Mycenae they extended the wall to take in a hidden staircase, cut down through the rock to an underground spring, so the citadel couldn't be cut off from water. You don't dig that unless you're expecting a siege.

Somebody moved those blocks. That was the Farmer, called up by the palace the way he'd been called up to dig canals in Iraq, and in the season when his own fields needed him. Later Greeks couldn't believe men had built those walls. They said it was the Cyclopes, the one-eyed giants. It was tired farmers and worried officials.

At Pylos, where the Counter has his desk, there aren't any walls like that. But go back to his shelf of damp tablets, because what's written on the last batch has changed.

There's one that orders the officials of every district to hand over bronze. It's specified as temple bronze, and it says what it's for: points for spears and javelins. They're taking metal from the shrines to make weapons. That's the Maker's world drying up. If new copper and tin were coming in on the ships, you wouldn't be melting down the gods' property.

There's a set that lists about eight hundred men, by name of their commander and by stretch of coastline. The heading says, roughly: how the watchers are guarding the coast.

And there's one that lists rowers, about six hundred of them, being called up.

It's the same desk and the same clay, and the same hand that used to count figs and wool. Now it's counting lookouts.

[VISUAL: Map — Cyprus and Ugarit; a cluster of ship icons between them with a question mark.]

And then somebody said it out loud.

In the archive at Ugarit there's a letter from the chief minister of the copper island, Cyprus. He writes in the proper diplomatic style, with all the courtesies in the right order. And what he reports is this. Twenty enemy ships were seen off the coast. They've done damage. They've now left, and he doesn't know where they've gone. He's writing, he says, so that Ugarit can be on its guard.

Look at what that letter is. It's the international system, with its scribes and its couriers and its formal greetings, being used to report that the international system no longer controls the sea.

On the Hittite side, the last Great King was doing something no Hittite king had done. He was fighting at sea. He had an inscription carved saying that the ships of Cyprus met him in battle three times on the open water, and that he destroyed them and burned them. It's the earliest sea battle that any surviving document describes. And it was fought by an army of highlanders in borrowed boats, off the coast of the island that supplied the world's copper, to keep open the lane their bread came in on.

He says he won.

[VISUAL: Object — a bronze ritual vessel from the tomb of Fu Hao, Anyang, China. Map: pull back to show the whole of Asia.]

Before the fire I want to pull all the way back for a minute, because none of this was the world.

In these same years, four thousand miles to the east, a queen of China named Fu Hao died. We know her name because it's cast into the bronze vessels in her tomb, and because the king's fortune-tellers wrote questions about her on bones: will Fu Hao's childbirth go well? Should Fu Hao lead the army against the western tribes? She did lead it. She was buried with nearly two thousand objects, and the bronze among them weighs more than a ton and a half.

In China the Bronze Age was at high noon. Nobody there had heard of Egypt or the Hittites, and nothing I'm about to describe touched them. What's ending isn't the Bronze Age. It's one region's version of it, the one that was tied together by ships and letters and brides.

[VISUAL: Part card update — THE FAMILY, 1200 BCE: the Keeper begs for grain and fights at sea; the Counter's totals are wrong; the Carrier's partners have stopped writing; the Maker is melting down shrine bronze; the Farmer is building walls and looking at the seed jar.]

So here's the family in the year 1200.

The Keeper is writing letters marked life or death.

The Counter has added it up three times.

The Carrier hasn't heard from his partner since last summer.

The Maker is melting the offerings from the shrine, because no ship has come.

The Farmer is hauling stone for a wall, and at home there's a jar he isn't supposed to open. His sister is on a ration list, and the rations are short.

Not one of them can help any of the others. Each of them is waiting for one of the other four to fix it.

The tree rings say what's coming next. Three thin ones in a row.
