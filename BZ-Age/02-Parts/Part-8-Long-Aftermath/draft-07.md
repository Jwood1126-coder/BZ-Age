## The Draft

---

[VISUAL: Part card — WHEN: about 1000 BCE, a hundred and eighty years after the fires. WHERE: Greece, then the coast of Lebanon, then the hills of Canaan. THE FAMILY: heading back under one roof.]

Somewhere around the year 1000 BCE a man is grazing goats under a wall.

He's in the south of Greece, below the hill of Tiryns. The wall above him is twenty-five feet thick and made of stones the size of oxcarts, fitted together without mortar. It's been standing empty since before his great-grandfather was born.

He knows who built it. Everybody knows. Giants built it. One-eyed giants, called Cyclopes, because no men could have lifted those stones.

He's wrong, and you know he's wrong, because you watched tired farmers build that wall two parts ago. They were his own ancestors, seven or eight generations back. But look at it from where he stands. Nobody he's ever met could build that. Nobody he's ever met has heard of anyone who could. A wall that size needs an explanation, and giants fit the facts he has.

It's Xenophon again, six hundred years early. When the knowledge goes, a story moves in. And archaeologists still call that kind of stonework Cyclopean.

So that's where we are. A hundred and eighty years after the fires, in a Greece that has forgotten its own palaces. I want to ask four questions about this world, and the first is the one that herder raises.

[VISUAL: Three figures in sequence — a clerk with a tablet; a young man with empty hands; a farmer. Caption: grandfather, son, grandson.]

How do you forget how to read?

It seems impossible. Once people can write, how does a whole country stop? But follow the Counter's line at Pylos and you'll see that nothing could be easier. I'm making up the family. The arithmetic is real.

The grandfather is the clerk at the desk by the palace door. He knows about ninety signs. He learned them the only way anyone could, by sitting at an older clerk's elbow in that room. There were no schools. There was nothing to read for pleasure. In all the Greek writing that survives from that age there isn't one line of poetry, and not one letter. The script was for accounts.

The palace burns when his son is about fifteen and half trained. The son gets away. He lives another forty years in a country where there's nothing to record and nobody who'd pay him to record it. He remembers some of the signs. He never uses them.

The grandson is a farmer. He's heard there used to be a palace. He's never seen a tablet, and nobody ever showed him a sign.

That's all it takes: three generations and nobody to practice on. A skill that has one employer dies with the employer. Greece would go four hundred years without writing anything down, and when Greeks wrote again it was in a completely different script that came from somewhere else.

Second question. Why didn't they just rebuild?

The stones were still there. The fields were still there. Plenty of people were alive who remembered how things used to run. Why not put it back?

Because a palace wasn't a building. It was an arrangement between specialists, and each one needed all the others to already be in place. Think of a town with one big factory. The factory closes. The machinists don't open a new factory. They can't. They'd need the suppliers and the customers and the bank and the rail line, and those all left when the factory did.

The Maker can't cast bronze without tin. The tin can't come without the Carrier's ship. The ship won't sail without a safe port at the other end. The port needs a king to guard it, the king needs chariots, and the chariots need the Maker. Take away any one piece and the others would have to wait for it. They were all taken away at once.

So people did the sensible thing. They got smaller.

Archaeologists count the places in Greece where people were living in 1250 BCE, and the places where they were living two centuries later, and the second number is about a quarter of the first. Now, that's a count of sites, and small poor villages are easy to miss. I don't believe three Greeks in four died. But a lot did, and a lot left, and the ones who stayed lived in hamlets of a few dozen, in houses of mud and thatch, and buried their dead with a pot and a pin.

[VISUAL: Reconstruction — the long building at Lefkandi. Objects: the bronze urn from Cyprus; the gold pendant. Date card: about 950 BCE.]

And yet it wasn't empty, and I want to show you the place that proves it.

On the island of Euboea, at a village called Lefkandi, a bulldozer cut into a low mound in 1980. Under it was a building a hundred and fifty feet long. Nothing that size was supposed to exist in Greece for another two hundred years. It had been put up around 950 BCE over two graves.

In one was a man's ashes, wrapped in cloth and placed in a bronze jar. The jar was an antique from Cyprus, already a century old. Next to him lay a woman with gold on her dress and at her throat a gold necklace that had been made in Babylon about a thousand years before she was born. In the pit beside them were four horses.

So in the darkest part of the dark age, somebody on that island was rich. They had things from Cyprus and from Iraq. They could get a hundred people to put up the biggest building in the country. And they buried their chief the way heroes get buried in a poem that nobody had written down yet.

The Keeper's line isn't dead, then. It's just shrunk back to where it started. And here's a detail I like a great deal. In the palace accounts at Pylos there's a title for a very minor official, a kind of local foreman. It turns up, among other places, in charge of small groups of bronze smiths. The word is basileus. The great lords who ran the palaces had a different, grander title, and after the fires that title vanishes. The foreman's title doesn't. By the time Greeks are writing again, basileus is their word for king.

The kings of the Greek dark age were the descendants of the palace's foremen. They were the highest-ranking people left.

[VISUAL: Map — the Nile to Byblos on the coast of Lebanon, with a stop at Dor. Object: the Wenamun papyrus (Pushkin Museum, Moscow). Date card: about 1075 BCE.]

Now I want you to meet a real person from these years, because there's one document that shows the new world better than any amount of digging.

Around 1075 BCE an official of the great temple in southern Egypt was sent on a business trip. His name was Wenamun. His job was to sail to the port of Byblos, on the coast of Lebanon, and buy cedar logs for the sacred boat of the god Amun. We have his report. Scholars argue about whether it's a real report or a story written in the form of one. Either way, somebody who knew that world wrote it.

You've heard of Byblos. Three hundred years earlier its king was the man who wrote to Egypt sixty times, begging for a few archers, calling himself a bird in a trap, throwing himself at Pharaoh's feet seven times and seven times more. Keep that in your head.

Wenamun's trip goes wrong at once. He stops at a harbor on the way, a town now run by one of the peoples who'd come with the sea raiders. One of his own crew steals the money he's carrying and runs. Wenamun goes to the local chief and demands his money back. The chief points out, quite reasonably, that the thief was Wenamun's own man. Nobody helps him.

He gets to Byblos with almost nothing. The prince of Byblos sends a message down to the harbor: get out of my port. He sends the same message the next day. He sends it every day for twenty-nine days.

Finally Wenamun is let in. He describes the scene. The prince is sitting in an upstairs room with his back to a window, and through the window Wenamun can see the waves of the sea breaking behind the prince's head.

And the prince asks him some questions. Where are your letters of introduction? Where's the ship Egypt gave you for the timber? What have you brought to pay for it?

Then he has his clerks bring out the old account books, rolls and rolls of them, and read aloud what earlier pharaohs sent in exchange for cedar. Six shiploads of goods, last time. He says: if the king of Egypt were my master, he would have sent silver and gold. I am not your servant. And I am not the servant of the man who sent you.

That's the prince of Byblos talking to Egypt. Three hundred years after the bird in the trap.

In the end Wenamun has to write home for money. Egypt sends gold, silver, linen, ox hides, rope, sacks of lentils, baskets of fish, and five hundred rolls of papyrus. The timber gets cut. And then, just as he's about to leave, eleven ships turn up from the town where he was robbed, with orders to arrest him. He sits down on the beach and cries. The prince, who by this point seems to feel sorry for him, sends down two jars of wine, a sheep, and an Egyptian singing-girl to cheer him up.

The papyrus breaks off before we find out whether he got home.

It's the best single document we have from the age after the collapse. The great power has to pay cash in advance, and the little port keeps the books.

One footnote. Those five hundred rolls of papyrus were Byblos's main import, for centuries. So much of it came through that town that the Greeks named the stuff after it. Their word for a papyrus roll, and then for a book, was byblos. That's where the word Bible comes from.

[VISUAL: Map — Tyre, Sidon, Byblos; trade routes spreading west to Cyprus, Sardinia, Spain, Carthage. Object: murex shells and purple-dyed cloth.]

That prince and his neighbors along the coast are the Carrier's line, and they came through.

They came through because they were small and they floated. Their towns had never carried the weight of a big palace and a chariot army, and they'd always lived off the sea. When the kings disappeared, there was nobody left to tell them where they could sail. Within two centuries the ships of Tyre and Sidon and Byblos were working the whole Mediterranean, as far as Spain. They planted a trading post in North Africa that grew into Carthage. They sold cedar, and a purple dye made from sea snails that was worth more than silver. The Greeks called them Phoenicians, which seems to mean the purple people.

So the Carrier doesn't work for a king any more. He works for himself.

Third question, and it's the one people argue about. Was the dark age dark?

[VISUAL: Map — the central hill country between the coastal plain and the Jordan valley; dots multiplying from about 30 to about 250. Illustration: a four-room stone house with a terrace and a cistern.]

Go to the Farmer, because he's moved. He's in the hills of Canaan now, the spine of high ground that runs down the middle of what's today Israel and the West Bank.

Before the collapse almost nobody lived up there. The towns were all on the plain, under local kings who answered to Egypt, and the hills were rough scrub with no springs. An Israeli archaeologist named Israel Finkelstein organized a survey in the 1980s, teams walking the ridges and picking up broken pottery, and the numbers came out like this. Before 1200 BCE, about thirty settlements in those hills. Two centuries later, about two hundred and fifty.

They're small. A dozen houses, mostly. The houses are stone, with a few rooms around a yard, and the ground floor is for the animals. There are pits cut in the rock for storing grain and cisterns lined with plaster to catch the winter rain. The hillsides are cut into terraces, which is backbreaking work.

And look at what isn't there. There are no palaces. There are no temples to speak of. There are no rich graves and hardly any imported goods. Everybody's pots are about the same.

Who were they? That's argued. Finkelstein himself thinks many were herders who settled down when the towns they used to trade with fell apart. Others think they were farmers from the plain who moved uphill to get out from under what was left of the kings. Their pottery is ordinary Canaanite pottery, so they weren't newcomers from far away. And that monument I mentioned from 1208 already has a people called Israel living somewhere in this country. Some of these villagers will become the Israelites of the Bible.

Now, here's the case for calling this a release. The Farmer keeps what he grows. For three thousand years, somebody has come at harvest and taken a share: the temple, the governor, the palace. Up here nobody comes. Terraces scattered over a ridge are a tax collector's nightmare. You can't see them from a road, you can't tell what they yielded, and it isn't worth the climb. His sons aren't called up to haul stone, and his sister isn't on a list.

And here's the case against, because I don't want to sell you a fairy tale. There's also no granary on the hill above him that opens in a famine. There's no garrison between him and the next valley, and the next valley is hungry too. The Bible's own book about these centuries is called Judges, and it's one of the most violent books in the collection: raids, massacres, a civil war. It has a line it keeps repeating. In those days there was no king in Israel, and every man did what was right in his own eyes. It isn't meant as praise.

So was it dark? From a palace, yes, because there's nothing there a palace would recognize. From the Farmer's doorway I think it was lighter and more dangerous at once. He was freer than his grandfather, and he had less of a net under him. People in that position usually want a king back within a few generations, and these did.

[VISUAL: Objects — Philistine painted pottery beside Mycenaean pottery; then a map of the five Philistine cities on the southern coast.]

Down on the coast below him, the other half of that story is settling in.

At a handful of towns on the southern plain, the pottery changes after the collapse. It's painted in the style of the Greek palaces, with birds and spirals. But it's made of local clay. That matters. Imported pots mean trade. Foreign-style pots made on the spot mean foreign potters who've moved in.

These are the Peleset, the ones Egypt fought and then settled. They brought their own cooking pots and their own kind of hearth. They ate pork, and you can see it in their trash, while up in the hill villages there are almost no pig bones at all. A few years ago researchers got DNA from the bones of people buried in one of those coastal towns. The earliest burials after the collapse have a clear dose of ancestry from southern Europe. Two centuries later it's faded to nothing. They'd married into the neighborhood.

In the Bible these are the Philistines, and they're the villains: the people of Goliath, the enemies of David. I find it oddly moving that both sides of that old quarrel came out of the same disaster. The hill farmers and the coast people were both washed up by the fall of the palaces. Neither of them knew it.

[VISUAL: Object — an iron knife with bronze rivets, Cyprus, 12th–11th century BCE. Diagram: a hillside with red iron ore; a simple furnace.]

Now the Maker, because he's found something to do.

You know his problem. There's no tin. He's been melting scrap, and every time he recasts it there's a little less.

On Cyprus, the copper island, the smiths had known about iron for a long time as a curiosity. In the 1100s they start making real tools out of it. The earliest ones are a nice thing to look at: knives with an iron blade, held to the handle by bronze rivets. It's the old metal and the new one in the same object.

Why then? The usual answer is that the tin stopped coming and they had no choice. I think that's partly right. But Cyprus went on making bronze through the whole crisis, so it can't be only that. I think it's also that the Maker was on his own. He had no palace telling him what to make and none supplying him, and iron has one enormous advantage that had never mattered before. The ore is everywhere. You don't need a ship or a treaty or a king, only a hillside, a lot of charcoal, and patience.

It wasn't better at first. Raw iron is softer than good bronze. But the smiths found, by accident and over generations, that iron left in a charcoal fire soaks up something from the charcoal, and if you then plunge it red-hot into water, it comes out harder than anything bronze can do. That's steel. By about 900 BCE they could do it on purpose.

Think what that means for the Farmer. Bronze had always been the king's metal, because only a king could get tin. An iron plow tip, an iron axe, an iron sickle can be made in any village with a smith. The best tools in the world don't have to come from the palace any more.

I said I'd tell you why the Iron Age began. It began as a substitute. People used the metal they could get. Then it turned out that the metal anyone could get was the better one after all.

[VISUAL: Sequence — the ox-head sign from Sinai; the Phoenician letter aleph; the Greek alpha; the Latin A. Then the same for house, bet, beta, B. Object: the inscribed ivory comb from Lachish.]

And the Counter. This is the fourth question and the best thing in this part. Where does the alphabet come from?

Remember the miners in the Sinai desert, around 1850 BCE, scratching an ox's head on a rock to stand for the first sound of their word for ox. I told you to hold onto it.

For six hundred years after that, almost nothing happens. The idea survives among the ordinary people of Canaan, used for scribbles: a name on a pot, a few words on a dagger. Every serious scribe ignores it. Real writing is Babylonian wedges or Egyptian pictures, and it takes twelve years to learn, and that's the point of it. It keeps the profession small.

There's one of those scribbles I love. In 2016 archaeologists in Israel found a small ivory comb, the fine-toothed kind you use for lice. In 2022 someone noticed faint letters scratched on its side. If they've been read correctly, and not everyone agrees, it's the oldest full sentence ever found in an alphabet. It says: may this tusk root out the lice of the hair and the beard.

The first sentence we have in the writing system you're using right now is a wish about head lice.

Then the palaces fell, and the schools that taught the big scripts fell with them. The little script didn't need a school. A father could teach it to a son in a few weeks. So in Canaan and on the coast it's the one that's left. By about 1050 BCE the Phoenician traders have settled on a standard set of twenty-two letters, and they take it with them wherever they sail, because a merchant needs to label a jar and write a receipt.

Around 800 BCE the Greeks, who've written nothing for four hundred years, pick it up from Phoenician traders. They keep the letters in the same order and they keep the names, although the names mean nothing in Greek. Alef, the ox, becomes alpha. Bet, the house, becomes beta. They add signs for vowels, which the Phoenicians did without.

So the word alphabet is two Canaanite words: ox, house.

From the Greeks it goes to the Romans, and from the Romans to us. From the Phoenicians it also goes east, to Hebrew and Aramaic and in time to Arabic. Nearly every alphabet on Earth comes down from those miners.

It's the most important invention in this whole series, and look at who made it. It wasn't the scribes with their twelve years of training, or the kings. It was a crew of foreign laborers who couldn't read. And what gave it its chance was the collapse. As long as the palaces stood, the hard scripts had all the jobs. When the palaces went, the easy script was the one still standing.

Here's a what-if that I can't prove and can't stop turning over. Suppose the palaces had survived. The clerks keep their hold on writing. The miners' alphabet stays a scribble at the edge of the desert. Bronze stays the king's metal, and iron stays a curiosity in a royal treasury. No farmer's son in the hills ever learns his letters. I'm not saying the collapse was a good thing. A great many people died in it. But some of what you and I take for granted grew in the ground it cleared, and I don't see how it grows otherwise.

[VISUAL: Map — Assyria and Babylon highlighted in the east: "never stopped writing." The west shaded: "forgot."]

One correction before we finish, so I don't leave you with the wrong map.

Not everybody forgot. In the east, Assyria, the merchant city, came through. It shrank. It lost its outer provinces and had some very bad decades. But it kept its capital, its kings, its gods and its schools. So did Babylon. In Iraq nobody ever stopped reading. Boys were still copying out the old Sumerian word lists, the same lists that bored boy copied.

That difference decides what comes next. The west lost its memory and had to make up stories about giants. The east kept its libraries. Hold onto that, because the last part begins in one of them.

[VISUAL: Part card update — THE FAMILY, 750 BCE: one stone house in the hills. A farmer who owns his field, an iron axe by the door, a jar with a name scratched on it.]

So where's the family, around 750 BCE?

Go back to the hill village and look inside one of those stone houses, early in the morning.

There's a man who works the field, and the field is his.

His sister is at the grinding stone, as she was in the first house. What she grinds stays in this house. Nobody has her on a list.

His grain is in a pit in the floor, and he's the one who decides when to open it.

When he needs an axe head he walks to the smith in the next village, who makes it from red rock out of the hillside, and he pays in olive oil.

Once or twice a year a trader comes up from the coast with a donkey, selling salt and cloth and news.

And on the shoulder of the big storage jar by the door, somebody has scratched a few letters. It's a name, his or his father's. Nobody in the house went to school. They just know the letters.

It's the first house again. The Farmer, the Keeper, the Counter, the Maker and the Carrier are back within shouting distance of each other, and most of them are the same person.

It's smaller than the world of the ship, and poorer. He'll never see blue stone from Afghanistan or hear four languages on a quay. He also doesn't need anything from the far end of the earth to get through the year.

And he has two things the first house didn't. He has a metal that doesn't need a king, and a way of writing that doesn't need a school. Out of those two things, in about three hundred years, his descendants and the Greeks' are going to build something new.

But they won't remember what came before. They'll look at the ruins and say giants. They'll tell stories about a war at a place called Troy, and about a flood, and about a baby in a basket.

So what did survive? And how, three thousand years later, did we find out what really happened? That's the last part.
