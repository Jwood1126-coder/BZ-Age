[VISUAL: Part card — WHEN: about 1000 BCE, a hundred and eighty years after the fires. WHERE: Greece, then the coast of Lebanon, then the hills of Canaan. THE FAMILY: heading back under one roof.]

Picture a herder below the walls of Tiryns, in southern Greece, around 1000 BCE. We are roughly two centuries after the destruction of its Bronze Age palace. The person is imagined; the massive walls are real.

The wall rises above him in huge, irregular stones fitted together without mortar. Some are as large as a loaded cart. The palace no longer operates as it once did, but people have continued living at Tiryns. A ruined royal center does not mean that every house around it is empty.

Later Greeks would explain walls like these as the work of Cyclopes, one-eyed giants. We cannot date that story to this particular herder's lifetime. But stand beneath the stones for a moment and you can feel the question that invites it: who could organize the work of getting something that heavy up there?

The builders were people, using human labor and organization. What later observers could no longer see was the working arrangement behind the masonry: the food, tools, supervision, and repeated effort. A wall can outlast the community's knowledge of how it was made.

It's Xenophon again, six hundred years early. When the knowledge goes, a story moves in. And archaeologists still call that kind of stonework Cyclopean.

So that's where we are. A hundred and eighty years after the fires, in a Greece that has forgotten its own palaces. I want to ask four questions about this world, and the first is the one that herder raises.

[VISUAL: Three figures in sequence — a clerk with a tablet; a young man with empty hands; a farmer. Caption: grandfather, son, grandson.]

How do you forget how to read?

It seems impossible. Once people can write, how does a whole country stop? But follow the Counter's line at Pylos and you'll see that nothing could be easier. I'm making up the family. The arithmetic is real.

The grandfather is a palace clerk. His script has about ninety syllabic signs, alongside signs for commodities and numbers. The surviving texts are overwhelmingly accounts. He learned a specialized practice supported by the palace, though we cannot reconstruct every detail of how scribes were taught.

In this imagined family, the palace burns while the son is still learning the work. He gets away, but has to make a living in a different way. He remembers signs he no longer uses regularly. No institution pays him to finish his training or teach somebody else.

The grandson is a farmer. He's heard there used to be a palace. He's never seen a tablet, and nobody ever showed him a sign.

That is one way to picture a break in transmission. The evidence shows the disappearance of Linear B administration after the palaces, not the thoughts of this invented family. When Greek alphabetic writing becomes visible centuries later, it uses a different system adopted from elsewhere.

Second question. Why didn't they just rebuild?

The stones were still there. The fields were still there. Plenty of people were alive who remembered how things used to run. Why not put it back?

Because a palace wasn't a building. It was an arrangement between specialists, and each one needed all the others to already be in place. Think of a town with one big factory. The factory closes. The machinists don't open a new factory. They can't. They'd need the suppliers and the customers and the bank and the rail line, and those all left when the factory did.

Rebuilding royal administration means more than repairing a roof. People need workers, supplies, customers, protection, and an authority they are willing or able to support. Some of those connections survive. Others have to be made again. A household can rebuild its own room without being able to reconstruct a kingdom.

So people did the sensible thing. They got smaller.

Archaeologists count the places in Greece where people were living in 1250 BCE, and the places where they were living two centuries later, and the second number is about a quarter of the first. Now, that's a count of sites, and small poor villages are easy to miss. I don't believe three Greeks in four died. But a lot did, and a lot left, and the ones who stayed lived in hamlets of a few dozen, in houses of mud and thatch, and buried their dead with a pot and a pin.

[VISUAL: Reconstruction — the long building at Lefkandi. Objects: the bronze urn from Cyprus; the gold pendant. Date card: about 950 BCE.]

And yet it wasn't empty, and I want to show you the place that proves it.

On Euboea, an island close to the eastern coast of mainland Greece, excavators at Lefkandi uncovered a very long building dating to around 950 BCE. Timber posts and mud-brick walls once stood above its stone foundations. Inside were exceptional burials. This was substantial construction in a period often imagined as nothing but tiny poor villages.

In one was a man's ashes, wrapped in cloth and placed in a bronze jar. The jar was an antique from Cyprus, already a century old. Next to him lay a woman with gold on her dress and at her throat a gold necklace that had been made in Babylon about a thousand years before she was born. In the pit beside them were four horses.

Someone here had access to wealth, skilled work, and objects whose histories reached across the sea. We should not turn that into an exact workforce or a complete biography of a chief. The burial and building are enough to interrupt the idea that every connection had gone.

The Keeper's line isn't dead, then. It's just shrunk back to where it started. And here's a detail I like a great deal. In the palace accounts at Pylos there's a title for a very minor official, a kind of local foreman. It turns up, among other places, in charge of small groups of bronze smiths. The word is basileus. The great lords who ran the palaces had a different, grander title, and after the fires that title vanishes. The foreman's title doesn't. By the time Greeks are writing again, basileus is their word for king.

The title changed its range of meaning. That is not proof that every later king descended from a palace foreman, or that the highest surviving official automatically inherited a crown. It gives us a trace of continuity within a changed political world.

[VISUAL: Map — the Nile to Byblos on the coast of Lebanon, with a stop at Dor. Object: the Wenamun papyrus (Pushkin Museum, Moscow). Date card: about 1075 BCE.]

Now go back about a century from that burial and travel southeast across the Mediterranean, to Egypt. A text set around 1075 BCE follows an official named Wenamun on a difficult journey. It will put a face, and a grievance, into this world of continuing trade.

Around 1075 BCE an official of the great temple in southern Egypt was sent on a business trip. His name was Wenamun. His job was to sail to the port of Byblos, on the coast of Lebanon, and buy cedar logs for the sacred boat of the god Amun. We have his report. Scholars argue about whether it's a real report or a story written in the form of one. Either way, somebody who knew that world wrote it.

You've heard of Byblos. Three hundred years earlier its king was the man who wrote to Egypt sixty times, begging for a few archers, calling himself a bird in a trap, throwing himself at Pharaoh's feet seven times and seven times more. Keep that in your head.

Wenamun's trip goes wrong at once. He stops at a harbor on the way, a town now run by one of the peoples who'd come with the sea raiders. One of his own crew steals the money he's carrying and runs. Wenamun goes to the local chief and demands his money back. The chief points out, quite reasonably, that the thief was Wenamun's own man. Nobody helps him.

The account then has him seize silver from people associated with the coastal group called the Tjeker, treating it as compensation for his loss. That matters later. The ships that pursue him are not simply an unexplained second disaster; he has created a dispute of his own.

He gets to Byblos with almost nothing. The prince of Byblos sends a message down to the harbor: get out of my port. He sends the same message the next day. He sends it every day for twenty-nine days.

Finally Wenamun is let in. He describes the scene. The prince is sitting in an upstairs room with his back to a window, and through the window Wenamun can see the waves of the sea breaking behind the prince's head.

And the prince asks him some questions. Where are your letters of introduction? Where's the ship Egypt gave you for the timber? What have you brought to pay for it?

The prince has records brought out showing what earlier Egyptian rulers supplied in exchange for timber. His argument is sharp: if he and his predecessors had simply been Pharaoh's servants, Pharaoh would not have needed to send payment. He rejects Wenamun's assumption that Egyptian prestige alone entitles him to the wood. The old records show exchange, not an earlier age of free cedar.

That's the prince of Byblos talking to Egypt. Three hundred years after the bird in the trap.

In the end Wenamun has to write home for money. Egypt sends gold, silver, linen, ox hides, rope, sacks of lentils, baskets of fish, and five hundred rolls of papyrus. The timber gets cut. And then, just as he's about to leave, eleven ships turn up from the town where he was robbed, with orders to arrest him. He sits down on the beach and cries. The prince, who by this point seems to feel sorry for him, sends down two jars of wine, a sheep, and an Egyptian singing-girl to cheer him up.

The papyrus breaks off before we find out whether he got home.

What I like about this account is that commerce keeps working inside the quarrel. There are ships, records, payments, and timber being cut. Egypt's standing is not what Wenamun thinks it should be, but the sea has not emptied and the little port has not forgotten how to keep accounts.

The surviving text breaks off after further trouble, before a completed homecoming. We can leave the missing ending missing. Its value is the world it describes, not a tidy proof that every old power has been replaced by a free merchant.

[VISUAL: Map — Tyre, Sidon, Byblos; trade routes spreading west to Cyprus, Sardinia, Spain, Carthage. Object: murex shells and purple-dyed cloth.]

That prince and his neighbors along the coast are the Carrier's line, and they came through.

Tyre, Sidon, and Byblos were coastal cities in what is now Lebanon. Their people are often grouped under the name Phoenicians. They had rulers, institutions, crafts, and trading networks; they were not towns that had somehow escaped all royal power. Over the following centuries their seafarers expanded their activities westward across the Mediterranean. Political change could create opportunities without making every merchant independent of a king.

The Carrier's work continues under new conditions, sometimes in partnership with rulers and sometimes in private ventures. The contrast is with the vanished palace network, not with government of every kind.

Third question, and it's the one people argue about. Was the dark age dark?

[VISUAL: Map — the central hill country between the coastal plain and the Jordan valley; dots multiplying from about 30 to about 250. Illustration: a four-room stone house with a terrace and a cistern.]

Go to the Farmer, because he's moved. He's in the hills of Canaan now, the spine of high ground that runs down the middle of what's today Israel and the West Bank.

The highlands had a history of settlement before the collapse. Surveys nevertheless show a marked expansion of small communities during the early Iron Age. The terrain made access to fields and water uneven. Springs, captured rainwater, storage, and the labor of working slopes mattered to where a household could live.

They're small. A dozen houses, mostly. The houses are stone, with a few rooms around a yard, and the ground floor is for the animals. There are pits cut in the rock for storing grain and cisterns lined with plaster to catch the winter rain. The hillsides are cut into terraces, which is backbreaking work.

And look at what isn't there. There are no palaces. There are no temples to speak of. There are no rich graves and hardly any imported goods. Everybody's pots are about the same.

Who were they? That's argued. Finkelstein himself thinks many were herders who settled down when the towns they used to trade with fell apart. Others think they were farmers from the plain who moved uphill to get out from under what was left of the kings. Their pottery is ordinary Canaanite pottery, so they weren't newcomers from far away. And that monument I mentioned from 1208 already has a people called Israel living somewhere in this country. Some of these villagers will become the Israelites of the Bible.

Here is the possibility that makes the idea of a release attractive. In some small communities, households may have had more control over their labor and produce than people attached to a palace estate. But a simple house and ordinary pottery do not prove that nobody collected dues, claimed land, or exercised power. We cannot read a tax exemption from the absence of a palace.

And here's the case against, because I don't want to sell you a fairy tale. There's also no granary on the hill above him that opens in a famine. There's no garrison between him and the next valley, and the next valley is hungry too. The Bible's own book about these centuries is called Judges, and it's one of the most violent books in the collection: raids, massacres, a civil war. It has a line it keeps repeating. In those days there was no king in Israel, and every man did what was right in his own eyes. It isn't meant as praise.

So was it dark? The answer changes with the place and the life we are trying to recover. Some people may have gained room to act while losing security or access to goods. Others faced displacement and violence. I find that uneven picture more convincing than either a universal disaster or a universal liberation.

[VISUAL: Objects — Philistine painted pottery beside Mycenaean pottery; then a map of the five Philistine cities on the southern coast.]

Down on the coast below him, the other half of that story is settling in.

At a handful of towns on the southern plain, the pottery changes after the collapse. It's painted in the style of the Greek palaces, with birds and spirals. But it's made of local clay. That matters. Imported pots mean trade. Foreign-style pots made locally can suggest people or skills moving, but imitation and exchange have to be considered too.

These are the Peleset, the ones Egypt fought and then settled. They brought their own cooking pots and their own kind of hearth. They ate pork, and you can see it in their trash, while up in the hill villages there are almost no pig bones at all. A few years ago researchers got DNA from the bones of people buried in one of those coastal towns. The earliest burials after the collapse have a clear dose of ancestry from southern Europe. In the later people sampled, that distinct signal was no longer detectable in the same way, consistent with mixing into the local population.

In the Bible these are the Philistines, and they're the villains: the people of Goliath, the enemies of David. I find it oddly moving that both sides of that old quarrel came out of the same disaster. The hill farmers and the coast people were both washed up by the fall of the palaces. Neither of them knew it.

[VISUAL: Object — an iron knife with bronze rivets, Cyprus, 12th–11th century BCE. Diagram: a hillside with red iron ore; a simple furnace.]

Now the Maker, because he's found something to do.

A smith in a disrupted community may have difficulty obtaining familiar supplies. Some bronze production and trade continue, especially in places such as Cyprus. The question is why iron becomes more widely useful alongside them, not how every smith instantly replaces a metal that has disappeared.

On Cyprus, the copper island, the smiths had known about iron for a long time as a curiosity. In the 1100s they start making real tools out of it. The earliest ones are a nice thing to look at: knives with an iron blade, held to the handle by bronze rivets. It's the old metal and the new one in the same object.

Disruption to supplies is one proposed part of the story, but it cannot explain the whole transition. Iron ores are more widely available than tin, while turning them into dependable tools demands fuel, furnaces, skill, and repeated work. Craftspeople in different places solve those problems at different times. A nearby ore deposit helps; it does not make a working iron industry appear by itself.

Early iron was not automatically better than good bronze. Heating iron in contact with charcoal can add carbon; controlled heating and cooling can then produce and harden steel. Simply plunging any piece of hot iron into water does not guarantee a superior blade. This is knowledge built through practice, and its adoption is uneven rather than one invention arriving everywhere in a particular year.

Over time, wider iron production changes the tools a farming household can obtain. That matters enormously. It does not mean bronze had belonged only to kings, or that every village suddenly possessed an independent smith with all the ore and fuel he needed.

That is the answer I can defend: changing supply networks, accumulated technical skill, and the wider usefulness of a different metal. The transition took generations. The name Iron Age makes it sound like a switch being thrown; the objects show people working across the change.

[VISUAL: Sequence — the ox-head sign from Sinai; the Phoenician letter aleph; the Greek alpha; the Latin A. Then the same for house, bet, beta, B. Object: the inscribed ivory comb from Lachish.]

And the Counter. This is the fourth question and the best thing in this part. Where does the alphabet come from?

Remember the miners in the Sinai desert, around 1850 BCE, scratching an ox's head on a rock to stand for the first sound of their word for ox. I told you to hold onto it.

Early alphabetic writing survives in scattered inscriptions over the following centuries. Their small number makes its users hard to reconstruct. It was not simply ignored by every professional scribe: remember Ugarit's alphabet written in wedges on clay, already used before the collapse. Different systems could coexist because they served different languages, traditions, and institutions.

There's one of those scribbles I love. In 2016 archaeologists in Israel found a small ivory comb, the fine-toothed kind you use for lice. In 2022 someone noticed faint letters scratched on its side. If they've been read correctly, and not everyone agrees, it's the oldest full sentence ever found in an alphabet. It says: may this tusk root out the lice of the hair and the beard.

The first sentence we have in the writing system you're using right now is a wish about head lice.

The political disruptions changed the institutions supporting different scripts. In several coastal communities, alphabetic writing continued and developed. A set of roughly twenty-two consonant signs became characteristic of Phoenician writing, carried through trade and other contact. Fewer basic signs can make learning the system easier; useful literacy still requires practice, and it was never automatically shared by every household.

Around 800 BCE the Greeks, who've written nothing for four hundred years, pick it up from Phoenician traders. They keep the letters in the same order and they keep the names, although the names mean nothing in Greek. Alef, the ox, becomes alpha. Bet, the house, becomes beta. They add signs for vowels, which the Phoenicians did without.

So the word alphabet is two Canaanite words: ox, house.

From the Greeks it goes to the Romans, and from the Romans to us. From the Phoenicians it also goes east, to Hebrew and Aramaic and in time to Arabic. Nearly every alphabet on Earth comes down from those miners.

For me, the alphabet is one of this story's most consequential inventions. Its early history shows people adapting an existing technology to their own language. Its later success involves traders, rulers, scribes, and communities, not just one profession losing its monopoly in a fire.

Here is a what-if. Suppose more of the palaces had survived. Would alphabetic writing and iron have spread through the same places at the same speed? Perhaps the opportunities would have been different. But both developments had histories before the destruction, so we cannot make catastrophe their necessary inventor. The interesting question is how people used changing conditions, not whether suffering had to happen to give us letters and tools.

[VISUAL: Map — Assyria and Babylon highlighted in the east: "never stopped writing." The west shaded: "forgot."]

One correction before we finish, so I don't leave you with the wrong map.

Not everybody lost the same institutions. Assyria and Babylonia, in what is now Iraq, retained kingship and cuneiform learning through periods of serious disruption and change. Egypt continued its own written traditions. These are different histories from the disappearance of Greek palace administration.

Those differences matter for memory. Some knowledge travels through schools and archives; some through stories, ritual, and practical work. In the last part we will ask what each route preserves, and what it leaves out.

[VISUAL: Part card update — THE FAMILY, 750 BCE: one stone house in the hills. A farmer who owns his field, an iron axe by the door, a jar with a name scratched on it.]

By around 750 BCE, several centuries after the palace destructions, this is no longer simply the first generation making do among ruins. New kingdoms and communities have formed. Trade has expanded in some regions. Greek alphabetic writing is beginning to leave us inscriptions.

Picture a farming household in that changing world. There is still grain to store and grind. Tools have to be repaired, and somebody has to deal with a trader or a person claiming a share of the crop. A mark on a jar may tell us that someone used writing; it cannot tell us that everyone in the house could read or that nobody went to a teacher.

We can recognize our five kinds of work without forcing them all back under one roof. Some are local. Others still reach across long distances. The old palaces have not returned in the same form, but neither has the world become a collection of households that need nothing from anyone else.

That is the world in which stories about earlier kings and their great buildings will be told, retold, and eventually written down.

So what did survive? And how, three thousand years later, did we find out what really happened? That's the last part.
