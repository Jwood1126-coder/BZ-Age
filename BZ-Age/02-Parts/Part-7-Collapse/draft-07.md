# Part 7 — The Collapse
## SEVENTH DRAFT — v0.7.2

Spine: the last years of Ugarit told through its letters in order, then outward city by city (Emar, Hattusa, Pylos), then Egypt's victory and what followed it, then the accounting for each line of the family.

## The Draft

---

[VISUAL: Part card — WHEN: about 1190 BCE. WHERE: Ugarit, the port on the Syrian coast. THE FAMILY: scattered across five countries, all in trouble at once.]

The last king of Ugarit was a young man named Ammurapi, and near the end he got a letter full of good advice.

You know Ugarit. It's the port where we stood at the end of a working day, with four languages on the quay and a woman singing in a doorway. It wasn't a great power. It was a rich trading town of maybe eight thousand people, on a low hill half a mile from the sea, with a palace of ninety rooms and its harbor in a bay of white cliffs just below. And it answered to the Hittites.

By about 1190 BCE its king had been getting orders from his Hittite overlords for years. We have them. Send ships to carry grain. Send your soldiers to serve with our army. There's one where a Hittite official tells him off for not turning up in person, like a boss writing to an employee who's missed a meeting.

He did what he was told. His infantry and his chariots marched north into Turkey. His warships sailed off to patrol the southwest coast of Turkey, three hundred miles away.

Then strange ships were sighted off his own shore. He wrote to the king of Cyprus, the copper island, an older ally he addressed as "my father."

[VISUAL: Object — tablet RSL 1 or RS 20.238 from Ugarit (National Museum of Damascus / Louvre). Text on screen as it is read.]

The answer he got is the letter full of advice. I'll paraphrase it. If it's true that enemy ships have been seen, the king of Cyprus says, then make yourself strong. Where are your soldiers and your chariots? Aren't they with you? Put walls around your towns. Bring your troops inside. Watch for the enemy and wait.

It's sound. It's also not much use, and the young king's reply explains why. We have his words.

"My father, the ships of the enemy have come. They have been setting fire to my cities. All of my infantry and chariotry are away in the land of the Hittites. All of my ships are off the coast of Lukka." That's the coast of southwest Turkey. And then: "Seven ships of the enemy have done this harm."

Seven.

Seven ships is perhaps three hundred men. A town of eight thousand, with one of the best harbors in the world, was being wrecked by three hundred men, because everyone who could fight was somewhere else, guarding somebody else's coast.

Who were the men on those ships? The letter doesn't say. Ugarit's scribes knew the names of the sea raiders of their day, and they didn't use one. So this next bit is me imagining, and I want you to know that it is.

Put yourself on one of those seven ships. The odds are you weren't born a pirate. Three years ago you had a farm, on an island or on the coast of Turkey, and it failed, the way farms were failing everywhere. You couldn't feed your children where you were. Somebody had a ship. And you've heard that the towns on the Syrian coast are rich and that their soldiers are away.

I can't prove that's who they were. But it fits something we'll see carved on a wall in Egypt in a few minutes, and it's closer to the evidence than the old picture of a horde from nowhere.

That's the answer to the second question I want to ask in this part, and I'll give it to you early. Why didn't the neighbors help? Because of how the whole thing was built. Ugarit's army was the Hittites' army. Its navy was the alliance's navy. The same arrangements that had made everyone rich had also scheduled everyone's soldiers to be far from home. When trouble came to every coast in the same years, each ally was waiting on the others.

For a long time a story was told about those last letters. It went that they were found in a kiln. The scribes had put them in the oven to bake them hard before sending, and the city fell before anyone took them out. It's a wonderful image, and you'll still find it in books. It isn't true. When archaeologists went back over the dig records, the "kiln" turned out to be a heap of rubble, with tablets in it that had fallen from an upper floor when the building came down.

I prefer the real version. The king's letter wasn't waiting in an oven. It's most likely the copy his office kept on file. So the plea was probably sent. It probably arrived. And it made no difference at all.

[VISUAL: Photo — the excavated streets of Ugarit; then arrowheads from the destruction layer.]

We know what happened next from the ground.

Ugarit burned. The layer of ash and collapsed brick runs across the whole site, six feet deep in places. There are bronze arrowheads scattered through the streets and inside the houses, which means the fighting went from door to door. And under the floors the excavators found little hoards: bronze tools, gold jewelry, small statues, wrapped up and buried by people who meant to come back for them.

Nobody came back. Ugarit was never rebuilt. A city that had stood for two thousand years was a hill with grass on it within a lifetime, and it stayed that way until 1928, when a farmer's plow hit the roof of a tomb.

One thing came out of that hill that I asked you to listen for. Among the tablets was a song. It's the hymn to the wife of the moon god, the kind that woman was singing in the doorway. It was written down a century or two before the end, and under the words there's a second set of signs, which most scholars read as instructions for the strings of a lyre. It's the oldest written melody in the world. The experts don't agree on how it should sound, and their versions are quite different from each other. But you can go and listen to them tonight. Ugarit's plea for help saved nothing. One of its songs got through.

So that's how one city died. Now the first question, which everyone asks. Who did it?

[VISUAL: Map — eastern Mediterranean with destruction dates appearing one by one over fifty years, not all at once; several sites marked "abandoned" or "not destroyed."]

The old answer was the Sea Peoples: one great wave of invaders rolling down the coast, burning every city in a single terrible year. I told you last time why I don't believe in the wave. Here's what the ground says.

It took about fifty years. A child born when the first palace burned could have died of old age before the last one did.

And it was a patchwork. A few years ago an archaeologist named Jesse Millek did something nobody had done. He took every city that the textbooks say was destroyed in this period, a hundred and fifty-three claims, and he went back to the original dig reports for each one. About six in ten fell apart. Some had been dated wrong by a century. Some had burned in one building and not the rest. Some had no evidence at all, only a line in one book that had been copied into the next.

So some cities burned. Some were emptied without a fight. Some carried on. And the ones that did burn weren't all burned by the same people.

Let me take you to three of them.

[VISUAL: Map — Emar on the great bend of the Euphrates, far inland. Object: a clay cast of a small child's footprint with cuneiform beside it.]

The first is a town called Emar, on the Euphrates in Syria. It's about as far from the sea as you can get in that country. No ship ever reached it. And it fell around 1185.

Before it fell its scribes left us the sound of a city going under, and it isn't screaming. It's paperwork. They kept drawing up contracts in the proper form, with witnesses and seals, and a group of those contracts all start with the same phrase: "in the year of distress."

In the year of distress, a man sold his house. In the year of distress, a man sold himself. One tablet records what grain cost while the town was under siege, and it's about a day's food for a shekel of silver, which was a month's wages.

And there's one contract I find very hard to read. A couple sold their children. Four of them: two girls and two boys, the youngest still nursing. The contract says why. It was the year of distress and they couldn't feed them. The buyer was a man with a full storehouse.

A child can't sign a contract or roll a seal. So the scribe took lumps of damp clay and pressed each child's foot into one, and wrote the child's name beside the print.

Those survive. You can see the toes.

The scribes of Emar had a set phrase for catastrophe because it had become ordinary enough to need one.

[VISUAL: Photo — the site of Hattusa, the Lion Gate. Map: central Turkey.]

The second is the Hittite capital, and it's the one that surprised the archaeologists most.

For a century people assumed it was stormed and sacked. More careful digging told a different story. The palace and the temples had been cleared out before they burned. There were no bodies and no treasure. The royal seals were gone and so were the statues of the gods.

The court had packed up and left. They took what a government needs to keep being a government: the seals that make a document official, the gold, and the gods, because you don't leave the gods. They left behind what they couldn't carry and didn't need, which was the past. Thousands of old tablets, treaties and letters and records going back four hundred years, still on the shelves.

Where they went, nobody knows. Some later time the empty city burned.

So the empire that had fought Egypt to a standstill didn't go down in a last battle. Its government walked out of its own capital one season and was never heard from again. And because they took their current files and left their archives, we can read four centuries of Hittite history and we can't find the ending.

One branch of that family did survive. Down on the Euphrates, in a city the Hittites had ruled through a line of royal cousins, the cousins carried on. Within a generation their king was calling himself Great King. The title was vacant, so he took it.

[VISUAL: Reconstruction — the palace of Pylos burning; then the baked tablets as found in the archive room in 1939.]

The third is Pylos, in Greece, and you know someone there.

Around 1180 BCE the palace burned, and nobody rebuilt it. We don't know who set the fire. It might have been raiders from the sea. It might have been the kingdom next door, or the palace's own hungry subjects. It didn't have walls.

Here's the part I told you to remember. The Counter's tablets were damp clay, meant to be wiped at the end of the year. They were on the shelf in the archive room when the roof came down in flames. The fire baked them. Clay that would have been mud by spring turned to brick, and it lay in the ruins for thirty-one centuries until an American team dug it out in 1939.

Everything I've told you about Pylos comes from that one fire: the figs, the weaving women, the bronze issued to the smiths. We have the last year's accounts of that kingdom only because the kingdom was destroyed.

And you remember what was in those accounts. Eight hundred men assigned to watch the coast.

Somewhere on that shore was a man on a headland, with a handful of others, looking at the water. We don't know if he saw anything. The tablet says where he was posted. It doesn't say what came.

The other Greek palaces went in the same decades. And there's a detail that complicates the picture of total ruin. At Tiryns, the one with the giant walls, the palace burned. But the town below it grew afterward. People moved in from the countryside and put up new houses in rows under the old citadel. They never rebuilt the palace, and they kept no accounts. They went on living there, without a king, for another hundred years.

[VISUAL: Relief — Medinet Habu: the sea battle; then the land battle with ox-carts carrying women and children. Date card: 1177 BCE.]

Now Egypt, because Egypt is where the wave finally broke.

In 1177 BCE the king of Egypt was Ramses the Third, and he's the one who left the famous description. It's carved on the wall of his temple, and it's the nearest thing we have to an ancient account of the whole collapse.

"The foreign countries made a conspiracy in their islands. All at once the lands were removed and scattered in the fray. No land could stand before their arms."

Then he lists the ones that had fallen: the Hittites, the south coast of Turkey, Cyprus, and others. He says the enemy set up camp in Syria and came on toward Egypt "while the flame was prepared before them."

He met them twice. There was a battle on land, somewhere on the coast road, and a battle on the water, in the mouths of the Nile. He'd lined the banks with archers and let the enemy ships come in close. Both are shown on the temple wall in huge detail. He won both.

Two things about those carvings.

The first is that his list isn't accurate. He names the Hittite cousins' city on the Euphrates among the fallen, and it hadn't fallen. A king's inscription is advertising. When it disagrees with the ground, I go with the ground.

The second is in the picture of the land battle. In among the fighting men there are ox-carts, heavy ones with solid wheels. There are women in the carts, and small children being lifted down out of the way of the fighting.

That's a migration. Those are families who'd put everything they had on a cart and walked south, because wherever they'd come from, they couldn't live there any more. I can't know what they'd seen. I think of the Farmer, though, and the seed jar. I think some of the people on those carts had been farmers in Turkey or on the islands, three bad years earlier.

Egypt didn't drive them all into the sea. It settled a lot of them in its own fortress towns on the coast of Canaan, as garrison troops, on rations. One group in particular, the Peleset. In a few generations they'll have five cities of their own down there, and their neighbors in the hills will call them Philistines.

[VISUAL: Object — the Turin Strike Papyrus. Then: CT scan image of Ramses III's mummy, the neck wound marked.]

So Egypt won. Here's what winning looked like twenty years later.

On the west bank of the Nile there was a village where the craftsmen lived who cut and painted the royal tombs. They were skilled men, paid by the state in grain. In about 1157 BCE their grain was eighteen days late. So they put down their tools, walked out past the guard posts and sat down behind one of the temples, and they wouldn't move.

A scribe wrote down what they said. We've come here because we're hungry and thirsty. There's no clothing, no oil, no fish, no vegetables. Write to Pharaoh about it.

It's the first strike in recorded history. They got some of their back pay, and then it happened again, and again. The richest state in the world could no longer reliably pay the men who dug its kings' graves. Within a generation those same villagers were on trial for robbing the tombs their grandfathers had built, and we have their confessions.

And the king who beat the Sea Peoples? There was a plot in his own household. One of his wives wanted her son on the throne. We have the court record of the trial, with the sentences. It doesn't say whether the plot succeeded. In 2012 his mummy was put through a CT scanner. His throat had been cut to the bone.

Egypt survived. It kept its river, its temples and its writing, which is more than most. But the empire in Syria was gone, and the gold that used to go north bought nothing now, because there was nobody to send it to.

[VISUAL: Object — the stele of Hammurabi and the Victory Stele of Naram-Sin side by side, as displayed in the Louvre. Map: arrow from Babylon to Susa in Iran.]

One more, far to the east, where no sea raider ever went. Around 1158 BCE the king of Elam, in southwestern Iran, came down on Babylon and ended the mountain dynasty that had ruled it for four hundred years. Babylon had no sea coast. Whatever was squeezing these kingdoms wasn't only coming from the sea.

That king of Elam liked souvenirs. He carried home the great monuments of Iraq's past. He took the victory stone of Naram-Sin, the king with the horns. And he took a seven-foot pillar of black stone covered in laws.

That's why Hammurabi's pillar was found in Iran. The first two empires of this story ended up as loot from the wreck of the third, side by side in the same storeroom. They're still side by side, in the same room in Paris.

So who did it? That's the wrong kind of question, the kind that looks for one culprit. The drought was real. The raiders were real. There were earthquakes, and revolts, and kings who made bad decisions. Any one of those, a healthy world could have taken. The horse kingdom's fall proved it.

What killed this world is that the parts had come to depend on each other so completely that they couldn't fail one at a time. A palace needed its smiths, the smiths needed ships, the ships needed safe harbors, the harbors needed armies, and the armies needed grain. Pull one out in a bad decade and the rest went down in order. The thing that made them rich is the thing that made the fall total.

A world of villages like the first house can't fall like that. This one could.

[VISUAL: Part card — THE FAMILY, 1150 BCE. Each line fades in turn as it is described.]

Now the family, one at a time, because each of them lost something different.

The Keeper is gone. There's no Great King of the Hittites. There's no king at Ugarit, or Pylos, or Mycenae. Egypt has one, with his throat cut. The men who called each other brother have no one left to write to.

The Counter has a skill nobody is hiring. In Greece his whole profession existed because one palace needed accounts. The palace is ash. There's nothing to count, no one to pay him, and no pupil to teach. He can still read. There's nothing to read.

The Carrier has ships, and no ports that are safe and no partners who answer. Some of his line, I suspect, are the people on the other side of this story now. A merchant captain with a crew, no cargo and a hungry family is not far from being a raider.

The Maker has no tin. So he does what you'd do. He melts down what's broken. A cracked pot, a bent blade, the hinges off a burned door. All over this world archaeologists find little stashes of scrap bronze from these years, weighed out and buried under a floor like savings. Every one of them belonged to somebody who thought things would get back to normal.

Chariots went with him, by the way. A chariot needs a stable, a workshop, a trainer and a king. Men on foot with javelins and long swords could deal with what was left of them.

The Farmer is alive. That's the first thing. There are fewer of him, and I can't tell you how many fewer, and I won't guess. But he never depended on tin or treaties to do his job. The collector who used to take a third of his barley hasn't come this year. In some places the Farmer put his family on a cart, and in some he walked up into the hills where nobody had ever bothered to farm. We'll follow him there.

And his sister.

[VISUAL: The ration tablet from Pylos listing women and children; the image slowly fades to blank clay.]

This is the third question, and it's the one I can't answer.

She's been in the story since the first house, at the grinding stone. Then in the weaving sheds of Uruk, as a number. Then in the lists at Ur, fifteen thousand of her. Then in the merchant's contracts, and on the ration tablets at Pylos, labelled "captives."

What happened to those women when the palace burned?

We don't know, and the reason matters. The only reason we ever knew they existed is that the institution that owned them kept a list. The list was the only witness to their lives. When the institution died, the list stopped.

Maybe some of them walked away free. That's possible. The thing that held them was gone. Maybe some died in the fire. Maybe some were taken by whoever burned the place and sold again somewhere else, into a ledger nobody kept. No document anywhere says.

For four thousand years they were counted and never named. At the end they weren't even counted.

I don't have anything to offer about that except to say it out loud.

[VISUAL: A dark coastline; a single fire on a hill. Slow fade.]

By 1150 BCE it's quiet.

The ships aren't coming. Nobody is writing letters in Babylonian to kings three months away, because there's nobody at the other end. From Greece to the Euphrates, people are living on what they can grow within a day's walk, defend with their own hands, and make from what's lying around.

And they're telling stories. In Greece the palaces are empty, and there's nobody left who can read the old accounts. But people remember that there were kings once, in great halls, with bronze armor and chariots, who sailed across the sea to a war. Nobody remembers the tax records. They remember the heroes.

That's all that's left of the Greek palaces for the next four hundred years: a story, getting better every time it's told.

The Hittites didn't even get that.

Next time, the world afterward. I'd always heard it called a dark age. I'm not sure that's the right word, and I'll tell you why.
