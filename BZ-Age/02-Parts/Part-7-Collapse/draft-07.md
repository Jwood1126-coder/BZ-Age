[VISUAL: Part card — WHEN: about 1190 BCE. WHERE: Ugarit, the port on the Syrian coast. THE FAMILY: scattered across five countries, all in trouble at once.]

The last known king of Ugarit was named Ammurapi. Among the letters from these troubled years is one full of good advice.

You know Ugarit from the imagined evening at its harbor. The capital stood a short distance inland from the Syrian coast; its port lay in a nearby bay. Cargo had to move between ship, shore, and city. The royal palace and archives belonged to a prosperous kingdom, but its king answered to the Hittite rulers in what is now Turkey.

By about 1190 BCE its king had been getting orders from his Hittite overlords for years. We have them. Send ships to carry grain. Send your soldiers to serve with our army. There's one where a Hittite official tells him off for not turning up in person, like a boss writing to an employee who's missed a meeting.

He did what he was told. His infantry and his chariots marched north into Turkey. His warships sailed off to patrol the southwest coast of Turkey, three hundred miles away.

Then strange ships were sighted off his own shore. He wrote to the king of Cyprus, the copper island, a ruler he addressed diplomatically as "my father." That title does not tell us the man's age.

[VISUAL: Object — tablet RSL 1 or RS 20.238 from Ugarit (National Museum of Damascus / Louvre). Text on screen as it is read.]

One surviving letter from the king of Cyprus offers advice. I'll paraphrase it. If enemy ships have been seen, make yourself strong. Gather your troops, secure your towns, and watch for the enemy. The letters do not come with a complete, securely dated sequence of replies, so we should not pretend we can follow the messengers day by day.

A letter from Ammurapi shows why such advice might be desperately difficult to follow. He reports enemy ships damaging his territory while his own forces are elsewhere.

"My father, the ships of the enemy have come. They have been setting fire to my cities. All of my infantry and chariotry are away in the land of the Hittites. All of my ships are off the coast of Lukka." That's the coast of southwest Turkey. And then: "Seven ships of the enemy have done this harm."

Seven.

Seven ships. The king gives us that number, but no reliable count of the men aboard. He reports attacks on his territory; he does not establish that these were the same crews that finally destroyed the capital. What we can hear plainly is his predicament: the forces he needed at home were serving elsewhere.

Who were the men on those ships? The letter doesn't say. Ugarit's scribes knew the names of the sea raiders of their day, and they didn't use one. So this next bit is me imagining, and I want you to know that it is.

Picture one possible man aboard a raiding ship: someone who once worked a field and can no longer feed his household from it. A boat offers work, escape, or the chance to take food from someone else. That man is imagined. He lets us consider a possible route into raiding, not calculate the odds that a particular attacker had been a farmer.

The families shown in Egyptian battle imagery will give us another reason to think about people on the move. They do not identify the men in Ammurapi's letter. Keep the possibility, and keep the two pieces of evidence separate.

Why didn't help arrive in time? Ugarit's obligations give us one part of an answer: a subordinate kingdom could be required to send troops and ships away. That made this coast vulnerable. It does not prove that every ally was helpless for the same reason, or that every surviving plea went unanswered.

For a long time a story was told about those last letters. It went that they were found in a kiln. The scribes had put them in the oven to bake them hard before sending, and the city fell before anyone took them out. It's a wonderful image, and you'll still find it in books. It isn't true. When archaeologists went back over the dig records, the "kiln" turned out to be a heap of rubble, with tablets in it that had fallen from an upper floor when the building came down.

The surviving tablet is not proof of a message trapped in an oven. A retained copy is one possible explanation for a letter remaining at Ugarit. But finding it there does not tell us whether a messenger arrived, what answer came back, or what help anyone attempted.

[VISUAL: Photo — the excavated streets of Ugarit; then arrowheads from the destruction layer.]

We know what happened next from the ground.

Ugarit suffered a violent destruction. Excavators found burned and collapsed buildings, weapons, and buried valuables. Picture what a collapsing upper floor can do to the rooms below: crush furniture, scatter stored objects, and cover an archive in rubble. That is a way to understand the remains, not a recovered account of fighting from one doorway to the next.

The great Late Bronze Age capital was not rebuilt as it had been. Its palaces and archives ceased to function. We cannot turn that into a statement about the fate of every person who had lived there. Centuries of later activity and weather would alter the mound before modern excavation began.

One thing came out of that hill that I asked you to listen for. Among the tablets was a song. It's the hymn to the wife of the moon god, a real surviving piece of music to set beside our imagined doorway singer. It was written down a century or two before the end, and under the words there's a second set of signs, which most scholars read as instructions for the strings of a lyre. It's the oldest written melody in the world. The experts don't agree on how it should sound, and their versions are quite different from each other. But you can go and listen to them tonight. The kingdom ended. One of its songs remained in the clay.

So that's how one city died. Now the first question, which everyone asks. Who did it?

[VISUAL: Map — eastern Mediterranean with destruction dates appearing one by one over fifty years, not all at once; several sites marked "abandoned" or "not destroyed."]

The old answer was the Sea Peoples: one great wave of invaders rolling down the coast, burning every city in a single terrible year. I told you last time why I don't believe in the wave. Here's what the ground says.

The changes unfolded across several decades, and not everywhere at the same pace. Different sites have different histories, and the dates are often less precise than a timeline makes them look.

And it was a patchwork. A few years ago an archaeologist named Jesse Millek reexamined a large body of destruction claims. He took every city that the textbooks say was destroyed in this period, a hundred and fifty-three claims, and he went back to the original dig reports for each one. About six in ten fell apart. Some had been dated wrong by a century. Some had burned in one building and not the rest. Some had no evidence at all, only a line in one book that had been copied into the next.

So some cities burned. Some were emptied without a fight. Some carried on. And the ones that did burn weren't all burned by the same people.

Let me take you to three of them.

[VISUAL: Map — Emar on the great bend of the Euphrates, far inland. Object: a clay cast of a small child's footprint with cuneiform beside it.]

The first is Emar, a town on the Euphrates in inland Syria. A river connected it to other places, but it was far from the Mediterranean coast. Its end in the early twelfth century cannot be explained simply by imagining ships beaching beneath its walls.

Before it fell its scribes left us the sound of a city going under, and it isn't screaming. It's paperwork. They kept drawing up contracts in the proper form, with witnesses and seals, and a group of those contracts all start with the same phrase: "in the year of distress."

Some of the contracts record people selling property or entering dependence during desperate conditions. Grain prices and the terms of particular transactions need to be read in their own documents. We should not turn a price from one tablet into an exact daily diet and monthly wage for every family in the town.

One contract records a couple selling four children, two boys and two girls, including a daughter still nursing. It names the parents and the children. The family explains its desperate need, and the transaction is recorded with witnesses. The people involved have not disappeared into an anonymous number.

The document says the parents pressed their children's feet into clay. Those impressions helped identify the children in the transaction. We should not invent a scene in which each child signs a contract or the scribe presses every foot. But the little clay footprints are real.

Those survive. You can see the toes.

The scribes of Emar had a set phrase for catastrophe because it had become ordinary enough to need one.

[VISUAL: Photo — the site of Hattusa, the Lion Gate. Map: central Turkey.]

The second is the Hittite capital, and it's the one that surprised the archaeologists most.

The evidence does not fit a simple picture of a full palace taken by storm. Important buildings had been largely cleared of valuable contents before they burned. Different parts of the city did not necessarily empty or burn together.

Someone had removed things worth carrying. We can picture the practical work of leaving: animals loaded, doors opened, objects selected and others abandoned. We cannot give that departure a precise day, an inventory of everything the court took, or a known destination. The archaeology suggests withdrawal and disruption rather than supplying a witnessed last procession.

The central government disappeared from its old capital. The political and cultural world it had helped create did not vanish everywhere with it.

At Carchemish, a city on the Euphrates ruled by a branch of the Hittite royal family, rulers continued to claim that inheritance. The name is worth keeping because it prevents us from confusing the end of the capital with the disappearance of every Hittite successor.

[VISUAL: Reconstruction — the palace of Pylos burning; then the baked tablets as found in the archive room in 1939.]

The third is Pylos, in Greece, and you know someone there.

Around the beginning of the twelfth century BCE the palace burned, and nobody rebuilt it. We don't know who set the fire. It might have been raiders from the sea. It might have been the kingdom next door, or the palace's own hungry subjects. It didn't have walls.

Remember the unbaked tablets in the palace archive. The fire hardened many of them. Working records that had not been made as a history book survived because of the building's destruction. The same event that ended the administration preserved much of what we know about its work.

Everything I've told you about Pylos comes from that one fire: the figs, the weaving women, the bronze issued to the smiths. We have the last year's accounts of that kingdom only because the kingdom was destroyed.

And you remember what was in those accounts. Eight hundred men assigned to watch the coast.

Somewhere on that shore was a man on a headland, with a handful of others, looking at the water. We don't know if he saw anything. The tablet says where he was posted. It doesn't say what came.

Other Greek palace centers suffered destruction and major change in these generations. But at Tiryns, the place with the massive walls, the town expanded after the palace burned. People built new houses and continued living there. That doesn't reveal every detail of their leadership or record-keeping. It does show why the death of a palace and the death of a community are different events.

[VISUAL: Relief — Medinet Habu: the sea battle; then the land battle with ox-carts carrying women and children. Date card: 1177 BCE.]

Now turn south to Egypt, where a king left his own very different account of these years.

In 1177 BCE the king of Egypt was Ramses the Third, and he's the one who left the famous description. It's carved on the wall of his temple, and it's the nearest thing we have to an ancient account of the whole collapse.

"The foreign countries made a conspiracy in their islands. All at once the lands were removed and scattered in the fray. No land could stand before their arms."

Then he lists the ones that had fallen: the Hittites, the south coast of Turkey, Cyprus, and others. He says the enemy set up camp in Syria and came on toward Egypt "while the flame was prepared before them."

He met them twice. There was a battle on land, somewhere on the coast road, and a battle on the water, in the mouths of the Nile. He'd lined the banks with archers and let the enemy ships come in close. Both are shown on the temple wall in huge detail. He won both.

Two things about those carvings.

The first is that his list isn't accurate. He names the Hittite cousins' city on the Euphrates among the fallen, and it hadn't fallen. A king's inscription is advertising. When it disagrees with the ground, I go with the ground.

The second is in the picture of the land battle. In among the fighting men there are ox-carts, heavy ones with solid wheels. There are women in the carts, and small children being lifted down out of the way of the fighting.

The carts and children suggest movement involving families, not only fighting men. They do not tell us exactly where each family began, what occupation it had, or why it left. I think of our imagined Farmer and his seed jar. That is one possible human story to place beside the image, not an identification supplied by the carving.

Egyptian royal texts also claim that captives were settled in strongholds and supplied with rations. Exactly where particular groups were placed, and how those claims relate to the later Philistine towns on the southern Levantine coast, is debated. The name Peleset is important to that discussion, but the texts do not give us a simple settlement map.

[VISUAL: Object — the Turin Strike Papyrus. Then: CT scan image of Ramses III's mummy, the neck wound marked.]

So Egypt won. Here's what winning looked like twenty years later.

On the west bank of the Nile there was a village where the craftsmen lived who cut and painted the royal tombs. They were skilled men, paid by the state in grain. In about 1157 BCE their grain was eighteen days late. So they put down their tools, walked out past the guard posts and sat down behind one of the temples, and they wouldn't move.

A scribe wrote down what they said. We've come here because we're hungry and thirsty. There's no clothing, no oil, no fish, no vegetables. Write to Pharaoh about it.

It's the first strike in recorded history. They got some of their back pay, and then it happened again, and again. The richest state in the world could no longer reliably pay the men who dug its kings' graves. Within a generation those same villagers were on trial for robbing the tombs their grandfathers had built, and we have their confessions.

And the king who beat the Sea Peoples? There was a plot in his own household. One of his wives wanted her son on the throne. We have the court record of the trial, with the sentences. It doesn't say whether the plot succeeded. In 2012 his mummy was put through a CT scanner. His throat had been cut to the bone.

Egypt survived, with kings, temples, writing, and a great agricultural base. It also lost much of its earlier power abroad and faced serious difficulties at home. The story is of a state under strain, not a country with no one left to trade with.

[VISUAL: Object — the stele of Hammurabi and the Victory Stele of Naram-Sin side by side, as displayed in the Louvre. Map: arrow from Babylon to Susa in Iran.]

One more case, far to the east. In the middle of the twelfth century BCE, Elamite rulers from southwestern Iran attacked Babylonia. A succession of campaigns ended the Kassite dynasty that had ruled there for centuries. This was a different political history from an attack on a Mediterranean port.

Elamite kings carried prestigious monuments back to Susa, their center in southwestern Iran. Among the objects taken east were the victory monument of Naram-Sin, the king with the horned helmet, and the great stone inscribed with Hammurabi's laws.

That is why these monuments of Mesopotamian kings were recovered at Susa in Iran. They had acquired a second life as another dynasty's trophies. Both are now in the Louvre; we do not need to put them in one ancient storeroom to understand what their journey means.

So who did it? A single culprit cannot explain these different endings. Some places were attacked. Some ruling institutions withdrew or failed. Environmental pressures mattered, but not in exactly the same way everywhere.

Connections could carry trouble: a force sent away, a shipment delayed, an institution unable to pay its workers. They could also bring help, alternative suppliers, and new opportunities. The evidence we have just followed is a patchwork of destruction, adaptation, and survival. Any explanation has to fit that patchwork.

[VISUAL: Part card — THE FAMILY, 1150 BCE. Each line fades in turn as it is described.]

Now the family, one at a time, because each of them lost something different.

At Ugarit and Pylos the old royal administrations are gone. Elsewhere kings survive, including successors in Egypt and the rulers of Carchemish. The Keeper's kind of work has not disappeared, but the person doing it, and the territory he can command, may have changed.

At a destroyed Greek palace, the Counter has lost the institution that sustained his work. That can interrupt the training of another generation. In Egypt and Mesopotamia, scribal traditions continue. The fate of writing depends on which rooms, patrons, and communities survive.

The Carrier may have lost a customer, a partner, or a familiar destination. Some routes become dangerous or unprofitable; others continue or change. I suspect some people moved between trade and raiding. We cannot give that biography to every captain whose name has vanished from an archive.

A smith whose supplies are interrupted can repair old objects and melt down scrap. Bronze working and exchange do not cease everywhere. A hoard tells us that metal was valuable enough to keep; it does not tell us the owner's exact plan or whether that person survived.

The loss of a palace can also damage the workshops, stables, and provisioning that sustain its chariot forces. Chariots themselves do not disappear from the ancient world. Their history continues under other rulers.

Many farming households survive; others are killed, displaced, or forced to change where and how they live. Losing a collector might mean keeping more grain. Losing a water supply, a market, or protection might mean the opposite. We need to follow particular places before deciding what the collapse did for ordinary people.

And his sister.

[VISUAL: The ration tablet from Pylos listing women and children; the image slowly fades to blank clay.]

This is the third question, and it's the one I can't answer.

The sister has been our way of keeping women's labor in view, from grinding grain to textile production. These are different women in different societies and legal positions. At Pylos, the ration records give us a glimpse of groups attached to palace work. They do not give us a continuous biography of the woman at the grinding stone.

What happened to those women when the palace burned?

For most of the particular women represented in those lists, we cannot follow what happened next. The records tell us what an administration needed to record, not everything there was to know about a life. When that administration stops leaving records, our view becomes much poorer.

Maybe some of them walked away free. That's possible. The thing that held them was gone. Maybe some died in the fire. Maybe some were taken by whoever burned the place and sold again somewhere else, into a ledger nobody kept. No document anywhere says.

Some ancient women and dependent people do survive by name. We have just met children named in an Emar contract. What we so rarely recover is their own account of the decisions made about them.

I don't have anything to offer about that except to say it out loud.

[VISUAL: A dark coastline; a single fire on a hill. Slow fade.]

By the middle of the twelfth century, the map has changed profoundly. At some ruined palaces the administrative record ends. In other towns people are building, trading, governing, and writing. Their lives do not all fit into the silence of an abandoned archive.

In Greece, memories of powerful kings and great buildings will enter stories passed from one generation to the next. Elsewhere, institutions carry older traditions forward more directly. The next question is what people can rebuild from the resources, skills, and relationships that remain.

Next time, the world afterward. I'd always heard it called a dark age. I'm not sure that's the right word, and I'll tell you why.
