## P7 destruction denominator: VERIFIED numbers, REVISE scope
Anchor: Part-7-Collapse/draft-07.md:68, note :250.

Millek's own book, *Destruction and Its Impact on Ancient Societies at the End of the Bronze Age* (2023), chapter 3, printed pp.53–55, explicitly analyzes 153 cited destruction events and rejects 94 (61%). He distinguishes misdated events, assumed destructions, and false citations; his assumed category includes small localized traces inflated into site-wide destruction. Thus six in ten is sound as HIS audit finding. It is not 61% of all Bronze Age settlements, nor proof that no upheaval happened. “Every city that the textbooks say” overclaims comprehensiveness and conflates sites and events. “Original dig reports for each one” is also stronger than what this passage establishes.

Suggested narration replacement for the audit setup: “He checked a hundred and fifty-three published claims of destruction around 1200 BCE against the archaeological evidence. In his assessment, about six in ten did not hold up.” The subsequent examples can remain.

## P9 Hrozný: VERIFIED sense, exact English quote NOT CLEARED
Anchor: Part-9-Inheritance-Memory/draft-07.md:166–178, especially :176 (“You will eat bread. And you will drink water.”).

Giulia Torri's scholarly SNS guide gives the Hittite sentence and the translation “You eat bread and drink water”. This supports bread/eat/water/drink and the 1915 Indo-European identification, but not the narration's wording as an exact historical English quotation.
https://mnamon.sns.it/index.php?id=54&lang=en&page=Lingua (Hittite, final introductory paragraph)

Elisabeth Rieken, “Hrozný’s Decipherment: Method, Success and Consequences for Indo-European Linguistics” (2017), section 2 discussion of Hrozný 1915:33f, analyzes both verbs as second-person plural present; she explains the comparison with Old High German ezzan and Old Saxon watar and the role of combinatory analysis and further verification. The grammatical present alone does NOT prove a future rendering impossible in context. Do not label the current future wording false; label its status as an exact attributed translation unverified.
https://www.academia.edu/39548638/2017_Rieken_Discovery_of_an_Anatolian_Empire_Decipherment

Suggested simple narration: “You eat bread, and you drink water.” Source-note this as a modern rendering, not Hrozný's verbatim English words. Keep no claim that one resemblance alone conclusively solved the language. Original 1915 article not inspected in this pass, so its exact German translation remains open.

## P9 Champollion: QUALIFY entire anecdote
Anchor: Part-9-Inheritance-Memory/draft-07.md:128.

Collège de France 2022 exhibition catalogue, introduction by Jean-Luc Fournet, printed p.6 (PDF page8), treats the arrival, exclamation, and collapse conditionally and attributes it to first biographer A. Rochas, *Notices biographiques et littéraires sur Champollion le Jeune* (1856), p.9. It quotes “Je tiens mon affaire, vois !”. This supports a translated “I've got it!” as the traditional report, not a verified contemporary transcript. Current script qualifies only the five days while presenting the rest as certain.
https://www.college-de-france.fr/sites/default/files/media/document/2022-09/Extrait-Catalogue-Champollion.pdf

Jean Capart's 1922 centenary lecture, printed p.5 (PDF page6), repeats the collapse and five-day crisis. This verifies that the five-day story has a documented scholarly retelling, not the event itself or its precise medical nature. It is not independent contemporary evidence.
https://tpsalomonreinach.mom.fr/files/original/ef9933b56c45335bac0eb5879747ef62809797e6.pdf

Suggested narration: “According to a later account, on a September day in 1822 he burst into his brother's office, cried ‘I've got it!’ and collapsed. The story has him recovering for five days.” Alternatively omit last sentence. Avoid claiming independently verified family testimony unless original chain is traced.

## P9 Rawlinson: PARTLY VERIFIED; precision/identity correction
Anchor: Part-9-Inheritance-Memory/draft-07.md:136; nearby :134 separate open issue.

Johannes Hackl's scholarly article reproduces Rawlinson's own 1851b:75–76 account in section 2. The Babylonian impression episode is dated autumn 1847. A Kurdish boy volunteered, was promised a reward, placed wooden pegs in clefts, used ropes, and made a swinging ladder seat to take the paper cast under Rawlinson's direction. Crucially Rawlinson says he had come from a distance: “local boy” is unsupported and should become “Kurdish boy”. The narrative compresses work from the 1830s through 1847; a small “Later” transition would help.
