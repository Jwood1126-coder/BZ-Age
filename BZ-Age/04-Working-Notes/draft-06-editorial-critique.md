## 1. Verdict

The series has a real spine, a real voice, and several sequences that are as good as anything in the genre. It also has one structural problem that explains almost every complaint in your own `Editor notes` (rough transitions, jumping around, ideas that do not weave, characters who arrive unexplained):

**The parts are built as tours, and the listener was promised stories.**

Each part opens on a person (Enheduanna, Lamassi, the bored student), leaves that person within three minutes, walks through eight to twenty topics in an order set by theme, and returns to the person at the end as a bookend. The people are frames. The topics are the content. Because topics have no natural order, the script moves between them with a bridge sentence each time, and the dates go backwards and forwards as it does. That is the jumping around.

The fix is the same in every part: choose one story that runs in time order from the first minute to the last, and hang each explanation on the moment that story needs it. Section 3 gives a candidate for each part.

Three other problems follow close behind: the script says many things three and four times; real documented people are outnumbered by invented stand-ins; and a few large pieces of the Bronze Age are missing or arrive only as an obituary.

## 3. The five structural problems, ranked

### 3.1 Tours where stories should be

Carlin's method is one event followed for an hour, with digressions hanging off it. Here the digressions are the spine. Part 2 is the clearest case: bored student, village recap, mammoths, the walk south, marshes, river freight, the temple, beer, Great Lakes, Cahokia, Caral, Poverty Point, writing, tokens, school, base sixty, the city walk, Inanna's statue, lost-wax casting, snapshot, disease, anonymity, slavery, sacred marriage, creation myths, the flood, Gilgamesh, cedar, the wall, Egypt, the Indus, Stonehenge, connectivity, urban share, the farmer, kalam and kur, the bronze blade, amber, the exit. Roughly forty topics in 11,600 words. Each is good. Together they are a syllabus.

### 3.2 The clock runs backwards

The dates of each section, in the order the listener hears them:

- **Part 1:** 5000 → 10,000 → 7000 → 9500 → 7500 → 8000 → 8000 → 7000 (Great Lakes) → 5000 → 3000 → 3200 → 4500 → 3200.
- **Part 3:** 2270 → 2334 → 2254 → 2200 → 2112 → 2004 → **2600** (the Royal Tombs, after the city has already fallen) → 2004 → 2560 (Giza) → 2160 → 2000.
- **Part 4:** 1880 → 1750 (Ea-nasir) → 1880 → 3500 (the wheel) → 2000 → 1650–1550 (Hyksos) → **1792** (Hammurabi, after his successors' century) → 1595 → 1550 → 1600.
- **Part 5:** 1300 → 1900 → 1600 → 1350 → 1270 → 1230 → 1274 (Kadesh).
- **Part 6:** 1259 → 1500 → 1340s → 1320s → 1300 → 1250 → 1208 → 1230 → 1210 → 1200 (China).

Two fixes. Reorder (the table in 3.1 does most of it). And give the listener a ritual: thirty seconds at the top of every part, same wording each time, stating the date, how far that is from the last part, and where on the map we are standing. Part 2 does this once at L301 and it works. Make it a fixture.

Specific moves:
- Royal Tombs of Ur (L803–805) → Part 2, where it supplies the dramatic set piece that part lacks, or to the *start* of the Ur III section as "what Ur had been". Where it sits now it interrupts the Lament.
- Chariot and Hyksos (L968–992) → after Hammurabi and before the Hittite raid on Babylon (L1036). The raid is then the chariot age arriving, in order, with cause before effect.
- The Egyptian widow's letter (L1374–1386) → Part 5, directly after Akhenaten. It is his family's story and it happened fifty years before the treaty that opens Part 6.
- Kadesh: tell it once. It is currently told at the end of Part 5 and again at the start of Part 6 (L1300 and L1346 are near-duplicates).

**Instructions to the listener.** The word *remember* appears 25 times and *hold* 30. "Remember that script." "Remember Ur." "Hold the name." "Remember him. Remember the shelf." "Keep your ear on that one." "Hold the question." A plant that works does not need the instruction; the Hurrian hymn would land without being flagged. Keep the instruction on four at most (the iron question, Mitanni, the shelf, the Indus script) and let the rest work silently.

**Verbal habits, by count:** "genuinely" 21; "honest/honestly" 16; "we don't know" 13; "four thousand years" 14; "three thousand years" 16; "what gets me" 3; "I keep coming back to" 3; "sit with" 5; "It's Tuesday / feels like Tuesday" twice in Part 5; "step around the crater" twice (L1108, L1790); "And yet." four times.

**Yardsticks.** The master plan asks for varied anchors. The Crusades are used four times (L176, L1150, L1712, L1764), Columbus three (L811, L1028, L1903), and a chain of grandmothers or mothers six. Suggested replacements are in §7.

**The delayed name.** Naram-Sin (L743–751), Hammurabi (L996–1002) and Akhenaten (L1168–1178) are each introduced as "he" for three paragraphs before the name is revealed. The master plan bans "bare withholding theater". Once is a device. Three times is a pattern the listener will hear. Keep it for Naram-Sin, where the reveal is the point ("and then he made himself a god").

**The accidental-discovery anecdote.** "We know about X at all because of an accident. In [year], a [local person] was [digging/plowing/diving]…" is told five times in the same shape (Göbekli L182, Mari L1012, Uluburun L1086, Amarna L1144, Ugarit L1552). Either cut two, or make it a deliberate motif and say so once: the Bronze Age was found by farmers, divers and gravediggers, and the professors came afterwards.

### 3.4 People: too many stand-ins, and names that arrive in lists

Your note says characters arrive without the listener knowing who they are. Both halves of that are true.

**Unexplained or under-explained on first hearing:** the Medes (L24); Kish (L521, explained only at L599); Lugal-ane; the Gutians; the Amorites; Elam; the Hattians; the Kassites; the Hyksos; Tiye; the habiru; Lukka (first heard inside a quotation at L1546 with no gloss); Muwatalli; Hattusili III (appears at L1348 as if known); Carchemish; Emar. Part 5's "table" (L1116–1136) introduces nine powers in two minutes, and the Sea Peoples paragraph (L1434) gives five names in one sentence.

Three remedies:
1. **Fixed epithets.** Oral poets solved this problem, and the series ends on Homer. Give each recurring name one tag and repeat it every single time: "Assyria, the merchant city"; "Mitanni, the horse kingdom"; "Alashiya, the copper island"; "Carchemish, the viceroy's city". The repetition is a feature in audio.
2. **Introduce by role, name second, and only if they return.** The decision log already adopted this (D-011). It has not been applied to the lists above. Sumu-abum (L996), Lipit-Ishtar (L1004), Tadukhipa (L1158), Eshuwara (L1508) and Özgüç (L901) can each lose the name and keep the role.
3. **One object per power at the table.** Egypt already has gold. Give Mitanni its horses, Babylon its lapis, Alashiya its copper, Mycenae its perfumed oil, and Hatti something (see §6: the thousand gods, or the treaty habit). Hatti is the one great power with no handle before Part 6.

**Stand-ins doing work that documented people could do.** Rule 11's archetype callbacks have started to manufacture history. At L1660 "the descendant of the Old Assyrian woman" watches "the correspondence with the distant trading colony" stop in 1180 BCE. That trade had ended five centuries earlier; the scene is built to serve the callback. The Part 7 roll-call (L1654–1662) and the Part 8 family (L1726–1740) are imagination presented in the same voice as evidence. Part 6's merchants "declining to risk what their fathers risked" (L1500–1504) is also inference with no flag on it. Part 8 contains no documented ancient individual at all.

The archives supply real people for every one of these slots. §6 lists them.

**The narrator.** "I" is present but has no history. Carlin's listeners know who is talking: a fan, not a historian, who gets obsessed and admits it. Two or three places where the narrator says how *he* came to a piece of this (the first time he read Lamassi; what he believed about the Sea Peoples before he read Cline) would turn the verbal habits above into a person.

### 3.5 What is missing

Ranked by how much the absence costs.

1. **How cuneiform was read.** Nine-tenths of the series rests on cuneiform tablets, and Part 9 goes from Layard's shovels straight to George Smith reading. The listener's obvious question (Rule 9), "how do they know what it says?", is never answered. See §6.
2. **Egypt as a lived place before Part 5.** The Old Kingdom gets four paragraphs as a "control case" with no person in it. The Middle Kingdom is skipped entirely. The New Kingdom arrives already formed.
3. **The Minoans.** They are introduced in their obituary (L1108). The listener is told a world ended before being shown that it existed.
4. **Where the alphabet came from.** Part 8 credits "merchants who wanted their accounts kept faster" (L1857). The evidence points somewhere far better for this series.
5. **Troy.** The Iliad is discussed in Parts 2, 7 and 9, and the site, the Hittite name for it, and the Hittite letters about it never appear.
6. **A Hittite scene before the decline.** We meet Hatti as a seat at a table and next as a famine.
7. **An answer.** The prologue hands the listener one question (L60). The ending offers "Rise. Plateau. Fall. Restart." and "we can see it". The series has earned four specific answers and should say them (§5, Part 9).

**New ones on offer:**
- For the Bronze Age's thin, essential supply lines: a modern chip supply chain, where one plant on one island makes the part everything else needs. Analytical, not just rhetorical: one input, few sources, no substitute, and nobody downstream can see the whole chain.
- For Akkadian as the diplomatic language: aviation English. Two pilots, neither a native speaker, talking in a third language because the system requires one.
- For Mitanni's absorbed fall: a bank failure the system survives, which teaches everyone the system is sound.

- For buffers hiding failure: living off savings. The household looks the same every month until the account is empty.

**Fresh yardsticks** to replace the Crusades and Columbus: the age of the printed book (about 570 years); the age of the oldest working universities (about 900); the whole run of Rome's empire in the west (about 500); "as far from them as the Vikings are from us" is used once and is good. ## 9. The one-shot (`the-cargo`) as a comparison

Skimmed only. Two observations that bear on the series:

1. It solves the transition problem with a device: every chapter opens from an object in the ship's hold (the figs, the writing board, the tin, the scarab) and returns to it. The listener always knows where they are and why the next subject follows. The series could borrow the device without borrowing the ship: one physical object per part, met in the first minute and held at the last. Candidates: the storehouse jar (1); the bevel-rimmed bowl (2); Enheduanna's own carved disc, found smashed at Ur and pieced back together (3); a letter still in its clay envelope (4); the ship (5); the treaty tablet (6); the footprints (7); an early iron knife with bronze rivets, which is the transition in one object (8); the cup (9).
2. Its ending ("Sign for it") is one gesture. The series' ending is three.

I am not recommending the one-shot's architecture over the series'. The series has the depth. It needs the one-shot's discipline about what the listener is holding at any moment.
